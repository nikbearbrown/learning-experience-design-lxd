## Opening Case: Nothing Happens in Week Three

*Illustrative case. The pattern — early-weeks attrition cliffs invisible to content outlines — is well documented in online-course research (see the Evidence Box); the course and numbers here are invented for teaching.*

The online project-management certificate had a respectable syllabus and a dropout problem nobody could locate. The analytics showed the cliff with brutal clarity: of every hundred students who finished week two, forty never submitted anything again after week three. Not a slow leak — a cliff, in the same week, cohort after cohort.

The course team did what course teams do: they studied the content outline. Week three was *Stakeholder Analysis and Communication Planning*. Nothing alarming. Not the hardest topic (week six's earned-value math, which everyone survived). Not the longest module. The videos were well-produced; satisfaction ratings from the students who *remained* were fine. The team concluded the topic must be secretly boring, commissioned a livelier video series for week three, and watched the next cohort drop 41%.

Then someone mapped the week as an *experience* instead of a topic. Monday: the course migrates from the LMS's built-in tools to an external collaboration platform — new account, new interface, a permissions bug that support takes two days to answer. Wednesday: the first group assignment opens — the course's first non-quiz deliverable, with no worked example, no rubric, and teammates the student has never spoken to. Friday: the first graded assessment of the entire course closes — before any student has received feedback on anything in three weeks. Three design decisions, made by three different people, none wrong in isolation, all landing in the same five days of a student's life.

The content outline showed a topic. The journey showed a wall: a tool change, an unscaffolded leap in task type, and a first assessment with zero prior feedback — stacked. No livelier video about stakeholder analysis touches any of it.

This is the week you learn to see the wall. The instrument is the learner journey map, adapted from customer-experience design, and its purpose is to show you the course your syllabus cannot: the one your learners actually experience, in time, with load, feeling, and friction left in.

## Core Content

### 6.1 A Journey Map Is Not a Content Outline

A **content outline** answers: *what will be presented, in what order?* A **learner journey map** answers: *what will be experienced, in what order — and what does each moment do to the learner's load, motivation, and likelihood of still being here next week?* The two describe the same course and share almost no information.

The method comes from customer-experience design, where mapping a customer's end-to-end path — touchpoints, actions, emotions, pain points — is mature practice (Kalbach 2016 is the standard treatment). LXD adapts it with one crucial substitution: where CX maps satisfaction and conversion, we map the constructs Act One taught you actually govern learning — load, motivation quality, feedback timing, productive versus destructive struggle. A journey map that only tracks "delight" is a CX artifact in an education costume, faithfully guiding you toward Chapter 1's engagement trap.

Three structural commitments make the learning version of the tool:

**Time is the spine.** The horizontal axis is the learner's lived sequence — including everything the outline omits: account creation, the wait for feedback, the gap between sessions, the return after a missed week. Learning-critical events disproportionately live in the omitted parts.

**Experience is multi-lane.** Under the timeline run parallel lanes (Section 6.2), so vertical alignment becomes the analytical act: when the load lane, the affect lane, and a tool-change touchpoint all spike in the same column, you have found something no single lane shows.

**Every cell is a claim with an evidence status.** This is the discipline this book adds to the practitioner method. Each entry is either *research-grounded* (a learner told you, or you observed it), *data-grounded* (analytics show it), or an *assumption* — and the map labels which, cell by cell. A journey map drawn from the design team's imagination is not research; it is the team's assumptions arranged attractively in time. The marketing-persona failure of Chapter 5 has an exact journey-map equivalent, and it is the most common artifact this course's instructors see.

**Design application.** Build the spine from the real calendar, not the module list. Populate lanes only where you have evidence, and leave visible gaps where you don't — the gaps are your instrumentation shopping list for Chapter 13.

### 6.2 The Lanes: Load, Affect, Friction, Reflection

What you track determines what you can diagnose. The course-standard lanes:

**Touchpoints and actions.** Every contact between learner and experience: content, tools, emails, people, support, assessment — including the *backstage-dependent* ones (a submitted assignment is a touchpoint whose other half is a grader — Section 6.4).

**Cognitive load.** Chapter 3 operationalized: where is intrinsic load high (genuinely complex content), and where is extraneous load being *added* — new interfaces, split attention, ambiguous instructions, tool fumbling? High-load moments are not automatically problems: high intrinsic load at a well-scaffolded moment is the job. Extraneous load stacked *on top of* high intrinsic load is the killer, and the map exists to show you the stacking.

**Emotional state.** Not decoration — achievement emotions have a serious evidence base. Pekrun's control-value theory holds that learners' emotions arise from appraisals of *control* ("can I do this?") and *value* ("does this matter?"), and that anxiety, boredom, and hopelessness have distinct, measurable relationships with learning strategies and outcomes (Pekrun 2006). So annotate *appraisals*, not emoji: Priya's "I failed the problem set and don't know why" is a control collapse; "math for the final" is a value blank — different emotions, different behavioral signatures, different fixes. Populate this lane from interview evidence and behavioral traces, never from how the design team imagines learners feel; teams reliably imagine their course is exciting.

**Friction.** Every point where progress requires effort beyond the learning itself — waits, switches, ambiguities, hunting for requirements. Logged neutrally; triage comes in Section 6.5.

**Reflection opportunities.** The lane designers most often leave empty because the outline never contained it: where does the experience ask learners to consolidate — retrieve, self-explain, connect to prior work? Mark both designed reflection (a prompt, a retrieval quiz) and *structural* reflection (a gap between sessions that spacing research suggests is doing silent work [verify — confirm the systematic review citation from the chapter notes before freeze]). A journey with zero reflection marks is a content delivery schedule; expect fluency without durability (Chapter 3).

### 6.3 The Dropout-Cliff Diagnostic: Frictions Stack

Here is the mechanism the opening case illustrates, and the closest thing this chapter has to a deep-dived law: **learners rarely quit over single causes; they quit where causes stack.** Attrition research in online learning consistently finds dropout concentrated in the early weeks, and trajectory studies show disengagement is patterned, not random — learners slide through identifiable states with inflection points that align with course events (Kizilcec, Piech & Schneider 2013; the persistence literature has made the structural version of this argument since Tinto 1993: departure is an interaction between the person and the institution's experience, not a property of the person).

Why stacking, mechanistically? Each friction consumes a budget: working memory (Chapter 3), motivational reserves (Chapter 4), and cost tolerance (Chapter 5's forgotten quadrant — frictions are its currency). Any one friction alone, the learner pays and proceeds. But the budgets are shared. The tool change spends working memory and time; the unscaffolded assignment then hits with no spare capacity and produces failure *without explanation* — a control collapse (Section 6.2); the graded assessment arrives before any feedback could repair control. For the learner whose value lane was already blank — Compliance Carlos; "math for the final" — no appraisal is left that argues for staying. Each designer made one reasonable decision. The learner experienced their sum.

This yields the chapter's central diagnostic, the one Objective 2 names: scan the map for **vertical pile-ups** — columns where multiple lanes go red at once — and treat the worst column as the journey's highest-risk moment. Two cautions keep it honest. First, a pile-up is a *hypothesis* about cause: the analytics show *where* learners vanish, the map proposes *why*, and the proposal awaits testing (Chapter 13 instruments it; Chapter 14 judges it). Courses are not randomized, and week three also coincides with everything else in a learner's life that happens three weeks in [verify — where possible, check whether the cliff replicates at the same *event* when the calendar shifts; that is the cheap observational test]. Second, absence of a pile-up where the data shows a cliff means your lanes are missing something — usually backstage (next section) or off-platform life. Say so on the map rather than inventing a cause; Hubbard's standard applies — the map is a measurement if it leaves you less uncertain than before, and honest gaps are part of how it does that (Hubbard 2014).

### 6.4 Service Blueprinting: The Actors Behind the Experience

A journey map shows the learner's side of the experience. A **service blueprint** adds everything behind it (Stickdorn et al. 2018): the **frontstage** (every actor and system the learner directly encounters), the **backstage** (the work the learner never sees — grading queues, content updates, administration), and the **line of visibility** separating them, with supporting **systems and policies** (LMS rules, late penalties, notification logic, staffing schedules) underneath.

Why a learning designer needs the backstage on paper: **backstage failures present as learner failures.** The canonical pattern: a course's analytics showed learners going silent mid-course — read by the team as classic disengagement, "non-completers." The blueprint told a different story: a support-staff transition had quietly stretched assignment-feedback turnaround from two days to nine. Learners submitted work, waited, submitted the next assignment blind, and concluded — reasonably — that nobody was on the other end. The relatedness need (Chapter 4) was being starved by a *staffing schedule*: on the journey map, learner abandonment; on the blueprint, a service-level failure with a precise, unglamorous fix. *(Illustrative case; the pattern recurs in service-design practice, the specifics are invented for teaching.)*

The blueprint also disciplines redesign ambition. Every frontstage fix has a backstage cost — "add individual feedback before the first assessment" is a sentence on a journey map and a staffing commitment on a blueprint. Designers who map only the frontstage routinely propose experiences their organizations cannot operate.

**Design application.** You do not need full service-design depth (this book borrows the method, not the discipline). The course-minimum blueprint: under your timeline, add three rows — frontstage actors/systems, backstage actions, governing policies — and draw the line of visibility. Then annotate every learner-facing promise (feedback, support, peer interaction) with who actually delivers it and at what turnaround. That single annotation pass catches most of what this section is for.

### 6.5 Friction Triage: What to Remove, What to Keep

The map is now covered in friction marks, and the naive next move is the dangerous one: smooth everything. Act One taught you why that fails — some friction *is* the learning. **Desirable difficulties** feel worse in the moment and produce more durable learning (Bjork 1994); strip them out and you get Chapter 1's beloved product that doesn't teach. So the chapter's final skill, and Objective 4, is **friction triage**: at each mapped friction point, decide *remove*, *keep*, or *keep but scaffold* — with the evidence status of the call labeled.

The triage tests, in order:

1. **Is the effort directed at the learning or at its packaging?** Struggling to retrieve a sampling concept is germane; struggling to install the statistics software is extraneous, full stop. Test: would a domain expert also pay this cost? The expert still installs the software (remove or smooth it); the expert does not struggle on retrieval (that struggle is the point — keep).
2. **Is the difficulty within reach, with feedback close behind?** Desirable difficulties are desirable only when the learner can eventually succeed and learns from the attempt. The same retrieval task is germane for the prepared learner and a control collapse for the unprepared one — difficulty is a property of the *learner–task pair*, not the task (Chapter 3's expertise caveat in journey clothes; the cognitive-load literature documents difficulty effects reversing for complex material and novice learners — Chen et al. 2018 [verify exact citation against the chapter notes' "undesirable difficulty effects" review]). Your personas are the instrument: triage each friction *per persona*. A problem set that is desirable difficulty for Priya may need scaffolding — not removal — for Carlos.
3. **Is it stacked?** A keepable difficulty in a red column is still a redesign target — not by removing the difficulty, but by *unstacking* it: move the tool change a week earlier, put a feedback cycle before the assessment, let the difficulty stand alone where the learner can afford it. The most evidence-disciplined fix on most maps is not subtraction but *redistribution*.

**Design application.** The triage output is a table your studio assignment requires: friction point → lane evidence → remove / keep / scaffold / unstack → evidence status. Expect to *remove* mostly logistics, *keep* mostly assessment-adjacent struggle, and *unstack* the one column where the cliff lives. And expect learners to disagree with your "keep" calls — they will vote every desirable difficulty off the island, which is next week's problem.

## The Evidence Box

**What the evidence supports, how strongly, and what remains unsettled.**

| Claim | Key evidence | Direction & strength | Unsettled |
|---|---|---|---|
| Journey mapping improves learning outcomes | — | **Honest status: no direct outcome evidence.** A practitioner method adapted from CX (Kalbach 2016); its warrant is instrumental — it surfaces experience-level problems content outlines structurally cannot represent | Whether mapped redesigns outperform unmapped ones has never been causally tested; the method earns its place by what it feeds into evidence-based decisions, not by being one |
| The constructs in the lanes govern learning | Cognitive load theory (Sweller; Chapter 3); control-value appraisals (Pekrun 2006); desirable difficulties (Bjork 1994); task-value/cost (Eccles & Wigfield 2002) | Strong — the lanes are where the chapter's evidence lives | Difficulty desirability reverses with expertise and material complexity (Chen et al. 2018 [verify]) — the boundary between desirable and undesirable difficulty is actively disputed in the CLT literature [contested — see pantry flag]; affect inference from behavioral traces is still maturing |
| Online attrition concentrates early and is patterned, not random | Disengagement-trajectory research (Kizilcec, Piech & Schneider 2013); persistence literature (Tinto 1993); low MOOC completion across platforms (Jordan 2014) | Consistent and replicated as description | **Causal attribution of a specific cliff to specific design events is the unsettled part** — observational alignment is not proof |
| Frictions interact (stacking) rather than acting independently | Mechanistically grounded (shared working-memory, motivational, and cost budgets); consistent with trajectory data | Plausible and design-useful; taught as a diagnostic heuristic | Direct experimental evidence isolating *interaction* effects of stacked frictions in authentic courses is thin — flagged accordingly |
| Backstage service failures present as learner disengagement | Service-design practice literature (Stickdorn et al. 2018); recurring practitioner pattern | Well-attested in practice; mechanism consistent with Pekrun 2006 and SDT | Prevalence unmeasured; a pattern to *check for*, not a base rate |

**Reading note:** the *method* this week is folklore-grade, while the *lanes* are evidence-grade. That combination — strong constructs carried by an unvalidated artifact — is the normal condition of design practice, and stating it plainly is the week's quiet second lesson.

## Worked Example: Mapping the DataWise 101 Dropout Cliff

**Situation.** The Track A case, continuing from Chapter 5. *DataWise 101*: eight modules, self-paced within a cohort calendar, ~55% completion. This week's analytics extract shows last-activity dates pooling sharply in week three: of students active in week two, 38% record no further submissions afterward. Chapter 5's research package is on the table — Priya and Carlos, the misconception finding sheet, the cost findings.

**The problem as the designer sees it.** The course team reads the analytics the way the opening case's team did: week three is *Sampling and Variability* — "the hard topic" — so the cliff is a content-difficulty problem, and the standing proposal is to soften it: shorter videos, an easier problem set, move the hard material later. Note the shape: a single-cause hypothesis with a smoothing remedy — one that would remove what may be the course's most necessary difficulty, the first real collision with Priya's stable-averages misconception.

**Process — including the dead ends.**

*Dead end 1: the prettier syllabus.* The first attempt put the eight modules on a timeline with emoji for predicted mood — the team's imagination arranged in time, nearly every cell ASSUMED. It could not show the wait for feedback or the software install, because nothing outside the outline had been entered. Discarded; rebuilt from the learner calendar and the Chapter 5 transcripts. (Reported because it is the default first attempt — including, probably, yours.)

*Dead end 2: frontstage only.* The second map was research-grounded and still missed the cliff's third ingredient. Adding the blueprint rows (Section 6.4) surfaced it: problem-set grading runs on a weekly TA batch cycle, and — the policy row — week three's submission volume coincides with a TA training handoff each term, stretching feedback turnaround to 8–10 days. Learners who failed the week-three problem set received the explanation *after* the week-four assessment closed. On the frontstage map, they just "went quiet."

*Building the real map.* Spine from the learner calendar; lanes from transcripts, analytics, and the course shell, each cell labeled research / data / assumption. Week three assembles itself into the now-familiar vertical pile-up: **(1) Tool change** — modules 1–2 run on embedded auto-graded quizzes; module 3 requires installing external statistics software for the first time (extraneous load spike; two transcripts document multi-hour install-and-error sessions). **(2) Task-type leap** — the first open-ended problem set, unscaffolded after two weeks of recognition-level quizzes, *and* the first content (sampling variability) that collides head-on with the stable-averages misconception — so intrinsic load is real and the wrong model makes early attempts fail in ways learners cannot interpret (control collapse, anchored to quotes from P2 and P5). **(3) First graded assessment** closes end of week three — before any problem-set feedback has arrived, per the backstage rows. Affect lane, evidence-grounded: anxiety for the Priya profile; for Carlos — value lane already blank — the column is simply the moment the cost stops being worth paying.

**Resolution.** The map replaces the single-cause proposal with a triage-and-unstack plan. *Keep:* the problem set's intrinsic difficulty — the course's first genuine collision with the documented misconception; softening it would protect the wrong model (the team's original remedy is overruled here). *Remove:* the software install from the critical path — move it to week one as a low-stakes guided setup task, when budgets are slack (redistribution, not subtraction). *Scaffold:* a worked example and a practice problem with immediate feedback before the open problem set — for the novice profile, the unscaffolded leap was load stacking, not desirable difficulty. *Unstack the assessment:* shift it one week later *and* — the blueprint-side fix — restructure TA batching so problem-set feedback precedes it; the line-of-visibility annotation makes this a costable staffing decision. Each call labeled with its evidence status; the whole map flagged as hypothesis pending Chapter 13 instrumentation (event-level analytics, next cohort).

**The lesson (one sentence).** The cliff was not a topic but a column — a tool change, an unscaffolded misconception collision, and a feedback-starved first assessment stacked in one week — and the map's verdict was to *redistribute* the frictions and keep the difficulty, the opposite of the smoothing fix the outline-level diagnosis demanded.



**The limit.** The map is an observational hypothesis built from five interviews, one term's analytics, and a blueprint walk — it cannot prove the stack causes the cliff (week three also coincides with add/drop deadlines and a tuition installment, which no redesign touches), and its per-persona triage calls inherit the existence-not-prevalence limit from Chapter 5. If the unstacked redesign ships and the cliff does not move, the map was wrong in exactly the way maps are wrong: it showed the frictions the lanes were built to see, and the cause lived somewhere else.

### Track B Extension: Mapping Your Own Project's Journey

Same method, your project, this week:

1. **Build the spine from reality:** the learner's actual calendar and path — onboarding, emails, waits, gaps, returns. If your project is short-form, the spine may be minutes and sessions rather than weeks; the method is scale-free.
2. **Populate lanes only from evidence you hold:** your Chapter 5 transcripts, empathy maps, and any analytics. Label every cell research / data / assumption. Empty cells stay visibly empty.
3. **Add the three blueprint rows** and annotate every learner-facing promise with its real deliverer and turnaround. An unknown turnaround is an assumption cell — usually the first one worth checking.
4. **Run the pile-up scan** and write the highest-risk-moment memo: the column, the lanes that go red, the evidence for each, the causal hypothesis stated *as* a hypothesis.
5. **Triage every friction** with the three tests, per persona. Objective 3 falls out of this: state explicitly where your map diverges from your content outline — the wall your syllabus doesn't show.

**Common Track B failure modes:** mapping the designed path instead of the lived one (no waits, no gaps, no support tickets); affect lanes invented rather than evidenced; a map with no assumption labels (which means everything is an assumption); and triage that removes every friction learners complained about — Chapter 1's engagement trap with better formatting. The Track B bonus (+5) requires the Evidence Disclosure to name one decision the evidence constrained and one it could not settle; the worked example's keep-the-difficulty call and its add/drop confound are the model.

## What Would Change My Mind

This chapter's load-bearing claim is the stacking diagnostic: attrition concentrates where frictions pile up across shared cognitive, motivational, and cost budgets, so *redistributing* frictions outperforms *smoothing* them. The finding that would force a rewrite: a well-instrumented multi-cohort study (better: a randomized rollout across sections) in which unstacking interventions — moving tool changes off critical weeks, inserting feedback cycles before first assessments, holding total difficulty constant — produce no reliable movement in early-weeks attrition relative to controls, while content-smoothing interventions do. If smoothing beats unstacking when both are properly tested, the pile-up scan is pattern-matching on noise, and friction triage should be rewritten to weight learner-reported difficulty far more heavily than the desirable-difficulties frame permits. The stacking mechanism is consistent with trajectory data and well-grounded constructs, but the interaction claim itself — the chapter's keystone — has thinner direct evidence than anything else taught this week, which is exactly why it is the claim named here.

## Still Puzzling

1. **The map's own validity.** Journey mapping has no outcome evidence (Evidence Box, row one). What would a fair test look like — mapped vs. unmapped redesign teams, same course, attrition as the endpoint? Nobody has run it; the field is teaching an instrument it has never calibrated.
2. **Affect at scale.** Pekrun's constructs are solid, but populating an affect lane currently requires interviews. Behavioral proxies for control collapse (rapid-fire resubmission? hint exhaustion?) are promising and unvalidated — Chapter 13 territory, unfinished.
3. **The unmappable causes.** Some share of every cliff is life, not course. Maps make design causes visible and thereby risk over-attributing to design what design cannot reach. How should a triage table represent the attrition the designer should *not* try to fix?
4. **Friction tolerance as a learner variable.** Cost budgets differ across learners and weeks, but we map friction as if it had one price. Per-persona triage is a crude first answer; a real model of who can afford which difficulty when doesn't exist in usable form.

## Chapter Summary

You can now make a learning experience visible as *time* rather than *topics*: build a journey map whose spine is the learner's lived calendar, with evidence-disciplined lanes for load, affect, friction, and reflection, every cell labeled research, data, or assumption; extend it below the line of visibility with a service blueprint that catches backstage failures masquerading as learner disengagement; scan for vertical pile-ups and write a highest-risk-moment memo whose causal claim is stated at its honest strength; and triage friction — remove, keep, scaffold, or unstack — per persona. You also gained this book's quieter discipline in its sharpest form yet: the week's method is practitioner folklore carrying evidence-grade constructs, and you can now say exactly which parts of your own artifact are which.

## Key Terms

- **Learner journey map:** a time-spined, multi-lane representation of the experience a learner actually moves through, as distinct from the content outline.
- **Content outline:** the syllabus view — what is presented, in what order; structurally blind to everything between the topics.
- **Lanes:** the parallel tracks under the timeline: touchpoints/actions, cognitive load, emotional state, friction, reflection opportunities.
- **Vertical pile-up:** a column where multiple lanes go red at once; the stacking diagnostic's signature and candidate highest-risk moment.
- **Control-value appraisals:** Pekrun's account of achievement emotions as arising from "can I do this?" (control) and "does this matter?" (value) (Pekrun 2006).
- **Service blueprint:** the journey map extended past the line of visibility — frontstage actors, backstage actions, governing policies (Stickdorn et al. 2018).
- **Line of visibility:** the boundary between what the learner experiences and the operations producing it; backstage failures cross it disguised as learner behavior.
- **Friction triage:** the per-point decision — remove, keep, scaffold, or unstack — made per persona with evidence status labeled.
- **Unstacking:** redistributing frictions across time so necessary difficulties stand alone.
- **Evidence status (of a map cell):** research-grounded, data-grounded, or assumption — the label that keeps a map a research artifact instead of imagination arranged in time.

## Bridge

The map now shows, with labeled honesty, where the experience fails its learners — the wall in week three, the feedback that arrives too late, the difficulty worth keeping. The obvious next move is to fix it *for* them. The next method asks you to resist that for one more week and redesign it *with* them — real learners in the room as design partners, with genuine decision power. And it hands you the dilemma your triage table just created: you have evidence-based reasons to keep frictions your learners hate. What happens when you ask them?

*Productive-struggle guardrail: this prompt requires your draft journey map and triage table (Exercise 6.3 work-in-progress) as input. An LLM asked to "make a journey map" will produce a plausible, generic one in seconds — every cell an unlabeled assumption: the exact artifact Exercise 6.1 teaches you to autopsy. Build yours from your evidence first; then use the model as the rival diagnostician.*

Then paste the following into the LLM of your choice:

```
You are a rival diagnostician reviewing a learner journey map for a
graduate LXD studio. I am pasting MY OWN journey map (as structured
text), my highest-risk-moment memo, and my friction triage table.
Do not generate a journey map of your own, and do not rewrite mine.

Your review, in four parts:

1. EVIDENCE AUDIT. For each cell or claim I marked research- or
   data-grounded, state whether my cited evidence supports the cell
   as written, or whether I upgraded an assumption. List every
   affect-lane entry lacking a quoted or observed source.

2. RIVAL HYPOTHESES. For my highest-risk moment, generate the three
   strongest alternative explanations that do NOT involve my proposed
   cause — at least one backstage/service explanation and one
   off-design explanation the course cannot reach. For each, state
   what evidence would distinguish it from my hypothesis.

3. TRIAGE CROSS-EXAMINATION. For my three highest-stakes triage calls
   (marked): argue the opposite call as strongly as the
   desirable-difficulties and cognitive-load literatures permit, then
   name which of my own personas is most harmed if my call is wrong.

4. THE MISSING COLUMN. Name the part of the lived journey my spine
   most plausibly omits (onboarding, waits, returns after absence,
   support), and what a red column there would look like in data I
   could actually collect.

Do not summarize my map back to me. Do not propose redesigns.
End after part 4.
```

**Assessable artifact:** submit (1) your original map package, (2) the LLM's review, and (3) a one-page response memo: which audit findings you accepted (cell relabeled), which rival hypothesis now enters your Evidence Disclosure as the unsettled decision, and which triage call survived cross-examination — with the evidence that held it. The memo is the graded part; a map that survives a rival diagnostician with its evidence labels intact is the week's definition of done.
