## Opening Case: Four Briefs, One Product

*Illustrative case. The product is invented; the four professional lenses are real, and the chapter sources their differences.*

A regional hospital network has a problem: new nurses are making medication-administration errors in their first ninety days, and the existing training — a four-hour slide deck with a quiz — is despised and demonstrably not working. The network's consultancy, in a fit of methodological honesty, assigns discovery to four designers from four traditions and asks each for a one-page brief.

The **instructional designer**'s brief opens with a performance gap analysis. The errors cluster in three task types; the training objectives don't match them. Deliverable: a redesigned curriculum — task-analyzed objectives, sequenced modules, practice items aligned to each objective, a criterion-referenced assessment. The brief is rigorous about *what must be learned* and nearly silent about what it is like to be a terrified new nurse at hour eleven of a twelve-hour shift.

The **UX designer**'s brief opens with usability findings. The training portal is six clicks from the scheduling app nurses actually live in; the quiz interface fails on their phones; search doesn't surface the med-administration protocol at the moment of need. Deliverable: a redesigned interface and information architecture, validated by task-completion and satisfaction testing. The brief is rigorous about *the artifact* and nearly silent about whether anyone learns anything from it.

The **service designer**'s brief barely mentions the training. It maps the medication-administration *service*: the pharmacy's labeling process, the interruptions at the med cart, the preceptor's role, the culture of not asking questions. Deliverable: a service blueprint of frontstage and backstage actors, with the training as one touchpoint among nineteen. The brief is rigorous about *the system* and treats learning as one lever it may or may not pull.

The **learning experience designer**'s brief opens with three days of shadowing and interviews. It describes the first-ninety-days arc as the new nurse lives it: pre-arrival anxiety, the firehose week, the moment around week six when questions start feeling too embarrassing to ask. Deliverable: a redesigned *experience* across that arc — spaced micro-practice tied to actual shift moments, a structured question channel that removes the embarrassment tax, just-in-time protocol access — with, and this is the tell, a proposed measurement: error rates and a delayed scenario assessment at day ninety, because the designer has read Chapter 1 and knows satisfaction scores will not answer the question the hospital is actually asking.

Four competent briefs. Four different deliverables. One product. The question this chapter answers: is the fourth brief a *discipline* — with its own question, methods, and standards — or is it just the first brief with empathy, the second with content, the third with homework? The honest answer is more interesting than either camp wants it to be.

## Core Content

### The Disciplinary Synthesis: What LXD Claims to Be

Plain language first: Learning Experience Design is the practice of designing learning the way experience designers design anything — starting from the human, mapping the whole journey, prototyping and testing — *with learning as the outcome that the design must demonstrably serve.*

The field's academic formulation comes from a small cluster of scholars. Jahnke and colleagues frame LXD as the integration of three dimensions — sociocultural (who the learner is, with whom, in what context), pedagogical (how the learning is structured), and technological (through what medium) — arguing that designs fail when any dimension is treated as an afterthought (Jahnke et al. [verify year]). McDonald and Westerberg (2023) offer the formulation this book finds most defensible: LXD is an *orienting guide* — it helps designers influence human learning by combining evidence about how people learn with human-centered design methods. Note the modesty of "orienting guide": not a theory, not a settled method, a stance. The practitioner formulation comes from Niels Floor, whose *This is Learning Experience Design* (2023) places the field at the intersection of design disciplines and learning sciences and foregrounds human-centered, goal-oriented, iterative design over the linear develop-and-deliver models that instructional design became associated with in practice.

For the teachers in the room, the design half of this synthesis may be the foreign part: "human-centered design" is a specific tradition — observe real users in context, define the problem from their experience, prototype cheaply, test with real people, iterate — not a synonym for being nice to learners. For the designers, the learning half is the foreign part: "evidence about how people learn" is also a specific tradition, the one Chapter 1 opened and Chapters 3–4 install, and it routinely contradicts design intuition.

What the synthesis does *not* include is its own original theory. LXD's theoretical pillars — constructivism, experiential learning (Kolb 1984), situated learning (Lave & Wenger 1991), cognitive load theory (Sweller 1988), self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan 2000) — are all imported. A recent bibliometric review (a systematic mapping of who cites whom across a field's literature) found that LXD has grown by borrowing from UX, instructional design, educational technology, and the learning sciences rather than by consolidating around a canonical theory of its own [verify — bibliometric review per synthesis; confirm citation before manuscript freeze]. Whether that is damning or normal for a young field is the question of the sections that follow.

**Design application.** When you assemble your studio project profile this week, the synthesis gives you the checklist of lenses, not answers: who is the learner and in what context (sociocultural), what is the intended learning and how is it structured (pedagogical), through what medium and with what constraints (technological) — and, the layer this book adds to every brief, *what is the current evidence the experience works* (usually: none, and writing "none" is the assignment).

### The Four-Field Comparison: Borders, Not Walls

The cleanest way to locate LXD is by what each neighboring field *optimizes for* — because methods travel freely across these borders, but optimization targets do not.

| Field | Primary question | Typical output | Optimizes for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Instructional design | What should be learned, in what sequence, with what practice and assessment? | Curriculum, modules, assessments | Performance efficiency — closing a defined gap |
| UX design | Is the interaction usable, useful, satisfying? | Interfaces, interaction patterns | Satisfaction and usability |
| Service design | How do touchpoints, actors, and backstage systems create a coherent service? | Service blueprints, journey maps | Systemic experience quality |
| LXD | What experience produces the intended learning — durably, for these learners? | End-to-end learning journey, prototyped and measured | Learning + motivation + usability, with learning as the constraint |

(Adapted from the field synthesis; the comparison logic follows Jahnke et al. and Floor 2023.)

Walk the hospital case back through the table and the briefs stop looking interchangeable. The ID brief is strongest on *alignment* — objectives, practice, assessment — the discipline's inheritance from its systematic, military-training lineage (the ADDIE family; Dick, Carey & Carey is the canonical text). The UX brief is strongest on *the artifact* — and "user experience" itself is a designed term, coined in the 1990s by Don Norman to name everything beyond the interface [verify — widely attributed]. The service design brief is strongest on *the system* — its blueprinting method (Stickdorn et al. 2018) sees backstage actors the other lenses miss; Chapter 6 borrows it outright. The LXD brief is distinctive in exactly two places: it takes the *experience over time* as the unit of design, and it accepts *learning as a falsifiable outcome constraint* — the design has not succeeded because it shipped, satisfied, or aligned; it has succeeded when there is evidence somebody durably learned.

**Design application.** Use the table as a diagnostic, not a tribal map. When a project is failing, ask which row's question is going unanswered. A beautiful course nobody finishes is usually a missing UX or service row; an addictive app that teaches nothing is a missing ID and LXD row (Chapter 1's entire subject); a perfect curriculum that dies at the med cart is a missing service row. The market reflects the blur: the same work is posted as learning experience designer, learning designer, instructional designer, and learning engineer, and the BLS-projected growth concentrates in workforce-tied roles, not in any one label.

### The Rebranding Critique, Taken Seriously

Now the uncomfortable section, which this book refuses to bury in a footnote. The critique, stated most pointedly in practitioner discourse by Devlin Peck among others, runs: *LXD is instructional design with better branding* [contested — see pantry flag]. Every definition of LXD emphasizing human-centeredness, iteration, and empirical testing describes what good instructional design has always been — Dick and Carey's model includes learner analysis and formative evaluation; ADDIE done honestly is iterative. On this view, LXD is what happens when instructional designers need to escape the compliance-training connotations of their job title, and the "new discipline" is a marketing artifact with a Medium following.

The critique deserves a fair hearing, and parts of it are simply true. The methods are not new: learner analysis predates personas, formative evaluation predates prototyping sprints, and nothing in the LXD canon improves on Sweller or Bjork — it cites them, exactly as ID does. The title inflation is real: employers use the labels interchangeably, and salary data for the two titles overlap almost completely. And the failure mode the critique warns against is common: portfolios full of empathy maps and delight language with no learning outcome anywhere in them — experience design *minus* the constraint, which is to say, the engagement trap of Chapter 1 wearing a lanyard.

What the critique misses, on the best current evidence: the *centers of gravity* genuinely differ. ID's institutional formation — performance gaps, objectives, efficiency — produces practitioners optimized for the first row of the table; the experience-design lineage produces practitioners who instinctively map journeys, prototype early, and treat emotion and context as design material rather than noise. The bibliometric evidence cuts both ways: it confirms LXD has no canonical theory of its own (point to the critics), and it shows a distinct, growing, cross-citing literature with its own venues, programs, and research questions — institutionalizing whether or not it is theoretically original (Jahnke et al. [verify]; the 2025 Purdue volume *Transdisciplinary Learning Experience Design* is the first edited academic volume treating it as canonical). Disciplines have been founded on less. UX itself was "just" human factors with better branding for a decade before anyone conceded otherwise.

This book's position, stated once and enforced everywhere: the disciplinary question is *empirically open* — and partly *unsettleable by evidence*, because "is X a discipline" is a sociological fact that hasn't finished happening. What would settle it: (1) method consolidation — do LXD-trained and ID-trained designers, given the same brief, produce measurably different processes and artifacts? (a runnable study nobody has run [verify]); (2) outcome differentiation — do LXD-produced experiences show different learning outcomes than matched ID-produced ones?; (3) institutional closure — degrees, journals, and job categories that stop being interchangeable. Until then, the honest label is the bibliometric one: **an emerging discipline, borrowing without consolidation**.

**Design application.** You will be asked some version of "isn't this just ID?" for the rest of your career. The evidence-disciplined answer is not defensive: "The methods are largely shared; the optimization target is the difference that matters, and I can show you what changes in the deliverable because of it." Then show the measurement plan. Nothing rebrands its way past a delayed retention test.

### Experience as the Unit of Design — and Evidence as Its Spine

The most defensible *positive* claim of LXD is about scope: the unit of design is the **experience** — temporal, social, affective, cognitive, and infrastructural — not the content, the artifact, or the session.

Each adjective picks out something a content outline cannot see. *Temporal*: learning happens across an arc — the syllabus shows week three, the experience contains the Sunday-night dread of week three. *Social*: the preceptor, the cohort, the embarrassment tax on asking questions — situated learning theory (Lave & Wenger 1991) holds that this is not the context of the learning but part of its substance. *Affective*: anxiety narrows attention and motivation decays on a schedule; Chapter 4 makes both designable. *Cognitive*: the load and difficulty machinery of Chapter 3. *Infrastructural*: the six clicks from the scheduling app, the phone the quiz breaks on. The experience-design tradition outside learning (Rossman & Duerden 2019) supplies the craft vocabulary: experiences can be *staged*, deliberately rather than accidentally.

Here is the danger, and the reason this book exists rather than just assigning Floor: an experience-first frame with no evidence discipline degrades into **vibe design** — optimizing the feel of the journey, precisely the variable Chapter 1 showed the market already over-rewards and learners cannot use to judge their own learning. The seductive-details and desirable-difficulties literatures are standing proof that experience quality and learning quality can be traded against each other *without anyone noticing*. So the book's definition adds the spine explicitly: LXD is experience design under a falsifiable learning constraint — every major design decision is an empirical claim about what these learners will durably gain, and the design is not done until the claim is checked or honestly labeled as unchecked.

That last clause is the course's grading mechanic, defined here formally. The **Evidence Disclosure**, attached to every studio assignment from Week 5 on, names which of your design decisions are *evidence-grounded* (the literature constrains them), which are *research-grounded* (your own learner research supports them), and which are *assumptions awaiting measurement*. It exists because the alternative — implying everything is evidence-based — is the portfolio that claims delight and shows no outcomes, in better dress.

**Design application.** This week's studio gate is the first experience-as-unit exercise: profiling your project means describing the *experience* (who, when, where, with whom, feeling what, through what infrastructure), not the content list. Most students discover the intended outcome was never actually specified and the evidence is a satisfaction survey. That discovery *is* the deliverable.

### Choosing the Studio Project: The Gate

This section is procedural, and it is load-bearing for the next thirteen weeks.

By the end of this week you select the learning experience you will carry through the entire course — researching it (Week 5), mapping it (6), co-designing it (7), prototyping a redesigned segment (8), auditing it for variability (9), making its motivation, modality, and AI decisions (10–12), instrumenting it (13), evaluating it (14), and assembling the portfolio (15). Skip a week and Week 15 has a hole shaped exactly like it.

**Track B (recommended): your own experience.** A course you teach or took, a product you work on or use, a training you can reach. The non-negotiable requirement: *access to 3–5 real learners* by Week 5 — not personas, not memories, people you can interview. Track B work earns a 5-point bonus per studio assignment, provided each Evidence Disclosure names one decision the evidence constrained and one it could not settle.

**Track A (always available): the instructor's case** — an introductory statistics online course with provided learner data, the case the worked examples in Acts Two and Three build on. Choose Track A if learner access is uncertain; you may switch tracks once, at the Week 8 checkpoint, without penalty.

**The profile (the ungraded gate, required for Week 5):** one page. (1) The experience, described as an experience — learner, context, arc, infrastructure. (2) The intended learning outcome, as specifically as the experience's owners can state it — quote them; the vagueness is data. (3) The current evidence of effectiveness, classified with Chapter 1's vocabulary: which engagement dimension does each existing metric measure, and is there any delayed or transfer evidence at all? (4) One sentence: why this experience is worth fifteen weeks. The most common gate failure: choosing a *content area* ("something about onboarding") instead of a *specific, reachable experience* ("my company's day-one-to-day-thirty engineering onboarding, whose owner has agreed to talk to me").

## The Evidence Box

*This chapter's claims are mostly about a field rather than about learning effects, so the evidence is bibliometric, definitional, and documentary — with confidence calibrated accordingly.*

**What remains unsettled:** whether LXD-trained and ID-trained designers produce measurably different processes, artifacts, or — the question that would actually matter — learning outcomes. ## Worked Example: The Portfolio That Claimed Delight

*Act One worked examples are diagnostic. Illustrative case — a composite of a genre every hiring manager in this field recognizes.*

**Situation.** Marcus, who will sit on your side of the table sooner than you think, is a hiring manager screening portfolios for an LXD role on a clinical-education team. The strongest visual portfolio in the stack is the pharmacology redesign from the checkpoint: rigorous-looking research artifacts, a genuinely lovely interaction design, satisfaction up 1.5 points, completion up 31%.

**The problem as the designer sees it.** Marcus's actual problem is a prediction problem: will this candidate's work *teach nurses pharmacology*, in a context where the failure mode is a patient harmed? The portfolio is optimized to answer a different question — is this candidate's work *liked* — and he needs a method for telling the difference through the portfolio's own surface.

**Process, including the dead ends.** First pass: Marcus tries to judge the artifacts. Dead end — the empathy maps and journey maps are competent, and (as the four-field table predicts) they would be equally at home in a UX, service design, or LXD portfolio; method artifacts cannot certify an optimization target. Second pass: the credential. Dead end — the candidate's title history (instructional designer → learning experience designer, same employer, same year) is the rebranding critique in miniature and settles nothing. Third pass: he applies the constraint test, the chapter's actual tool — three questions any LXD claimant should survive. (1) *What was the intended learning outcome, stated before design began?* The portfolio never says — the outcome appears retroactively as "learners loved the new experience." (2) *Which design decisions were constrained by learning evidence?* One promising candidate — the redesign spread the practice quizzes across the module instead of massing them at the end — but the portfolio narrates it as "reducing end-of-course stress": the spacing was a side effect of an affective goal, not an evidence-grounded decision. The distinction matters and the portfolio cannot make it, because it lacks the disclosure habit. (3) *What would the designer measure to know if it worked, and did they?* Satisfaction and completion — in Chapter 1 vocabulary, behavioral and affective engagement, no delayed or transfer measure, on content where transfer is the entire point.

**Resolution.** Marcus does not reject the candidate — the craft is real, and craft is necessary. He writes one interview question instead: "Walk me through one decision in this project where evidence about learning overruled what learners or stakeholders wanted." The candidate has no such decision and does not understand why the question matters; a second candidate, with a plainer portfolio, answers instantly — a story about refusing to remove retrieval practice, the satisfaction hit she took for it, and what she measured to defend it. He hires the second candidate.

**The lesson.** A portfolio — like a product, like a discipline — is classified by the outcome it can show evidence for, not by the methods it displays.

**The limit.** The constraint test detects the *absence* of evidence discipline; it cannot detect competence ceilings, collaboration, ethics, or whether the first candidate would thrive with one good mentor — in a field this young, punishing practitioners for the gaps in their training rather than their judgment is unjust and self-defeating. Diagnostic tools size people's artifacts, not people. The same restraint applies when you run this test on your own pre-course portfolio.

## Evidence Disclosure

**Constrained:** The decision to present the rebranding critique inside the chapter, at full strength, rather than relegating it to a footnote. The constraint here is documentary rather than experimental: the critique is prominent in the field's own discourse, the bibliometric evidence genuinely shows borrowing without theoretical consolidation, and a textbook that hid this would fail its own Chapter 1 test — claiming an authority the evidence does not support. Honest framing was the only framing the book's thesis permits.

**Could not settle:** Whether LXD's optimization-target distinction produces *different designs in practice* — the chapter's positive case rests on definitional analysis and practitioner-formation arguments, not on comparative studies, because none exist [verify]. The book proceeds as if the distinction is real and teachable while disclosing that its strongest validation would be a study nobody has run. Track that claim; if you are still in this field in ten years, you may be the one who runs it.

## What Would Change My Mind

This chapter's central claim is that LXD is meaningfully characterized — and distinguished from its neighbors — by treating learning as a falsifiable outcome constraint on experience design. The claim would require revision if a well-designed comparative study found that designers trained and labeled as LXD practitioners were *no more likely* than UX-trained designers to specify learning outcomes, choose delayed or transfer measures, or alter designs in response to learning evidence — if the optimization-target difference lived entirely in textbooks and not at all in practice. (A finding that ID-trained designers behave identically to LXD-trained ones would be less damaging: it would vindicate the rebranding critique's genealogy while leaving the chapter's definition of the practice intact — the label would be redundant, not wrong.) If the constraint does not survive contact with practitioners, the field is what its harshest critics say it is, and the second edition of this chapter would need retitling.

## Still Puzzling

- **Does the field need its own theory at all?** Engineering borrowed all its physics and nobody minds. "Borrowing without consolidation" is presented here as a mark against disciplinary maturity — but it may simply be what an applied design field permanently looks like.
- **Is the title inflation harmless?** If "LXD" commands more respect and budget than "ID" for the same work, the relabeling may buy practitioners the standing to *do* the rigorous work. Or it may be the engagement trap operating on the profession itself: selected for appeal, unaccountable for outcomes. The chapter cannot tell which, and suspects both.
- **Who owns the learning constraint when the LX designer doesn't?** In real org charts, the measurement belongs to a data team, the outcome to a business owner, the learner relationship to no one. Whether a discipline can be defined by a constraint its practitioners often lack the authority to enforce is an open — and rather pointed — question.

## Key Terms

- **Learning Experience Design (LXD)** — human-centered design of learning experiences with learning as a falsifiable outcome constraint; honestly framed as an emerging discipline.
- **Instructional design (ID)** — the systematic design of instruction to close defined performance gaps; LXD's nearest neighbor and alleged prior identity.
- **Human-centered design** — the design tradition of researching real users in context, prototyping cheaply, and testing iteratively; one of LXD's two parents.
- **Optimization target** — what a design practice ultimately maximizes and is willing to trade other goods for; this book's tool for classifying fields and portfolios.
- **Service blueprint** — service design's map of frontstage and backstage actors and processes behind an experience; borrowed in Chapter 6.
- **Experience (as unit of design)** — the temporal, social, affective, cognitive, and infrastructural arc the learner actually lives, as distinct from the content outline.
- **Vibe design** — experience design without the learning constraint; the engagement trap practiced professionally. (This book's coinage; use it sparingly and only about work, never people.)
- **Evidence Disclosure** — the course's standing artifact: every design decision labeled evidence-grounded, research-grounded, or assumption awaiting measurement.
- **Rebranding critique** — the contested claim that LXD is instructional design renamed; partially true about methods and labels, unresolved about practice.
- **Track A / Track B** — the studio paths: instructor's statistics course with provided data, or the student's own experience with real learner access.

## Bridge

LXD's entire claim to distinctness rests on the learning constraint — the design must demonstrably produce learning. A constraint you cannot operate is a slogan: to enforce it, the designer must know how learning actually works, mechanically, at the level where design decisions either respect the machinery or break it. Two weeks of machinery follow. Chapter 3 opens with the cognitive architecture — load, difficulty, and durable learning — and with a redesign that made a lesson smoother, prettier, easier, and worse.
