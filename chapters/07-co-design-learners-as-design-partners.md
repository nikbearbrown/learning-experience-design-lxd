## Opening Case: The Room Votes to Remove the Practice Tests

*This case is illustrative — a composite assembled from documented co-design failure modes and the metacognition literature cited below. No single session is being reported.*

The whiteboard wall is covered in sticky notes. Eight learners — working nurses, six weeks into an online pharmacology refresher — have spent ninety minutes doing exactly what the facilitator hoped: annotating the journey map with moments the designer never saw. The module that loads badly on a phone during a night-shift break. The discussion forum nobody reads. The calculation walkthrough one learner calls "the only part that respects my time." The session is working. The room trusts itself.

Then the facilitator asks the prepared closing question: "If you could change one thing about this course, what would it be?"

A learner near the window says it first: "The weekly quizzes. Get rid of them." Around the table, heads nod. The quizzes feel like surveillance, someone says. They make a hard week harder. One learner — the strongest student in the room, by the course data — makes the case crisply: "I learn from the videos and the walkthroughs. The quizzes just test me on what I already learned. They're not teaching me anything. They're stress."

The facilitator, trained to honor participant voice, writes *REMOVE WEEKLY QUIZZES* on the board and circles it. The room applauds, lightly. It is the session's most unanimous finding.

It is also, by the weight of available evidence, the session's most reliably wrong one. The quizzes are retrieval practice — among the best-replicated interventions in learning science, with benefits for delayed retention demonstrated across hundreds of studies (Roediger & Karpicke 2006). And the learner's self-report — "they're not teaching me anything" — is precisely what the metacognition literature predicts a learner *will* say about retrieval practice, because the act of effortful recall feels like assessment of learning rather than the production of it (Soderstrom & Bjork 2015).

Here is the designer's dilemma in one room: **the learners are the experts on their experience, and they are wrong about their learning.** Both halves of that sentence are true at once. This chapter teaches you to design with that sentence — not around it.

## Core Content

### What Co-Design Is — and What It Is Not

**Co-design** (used here interchangeably with participatory design in its design-method sense) means engaging learners as partners in framing problems and generating solutions — not merely as sources of reactions to designs that are already finished. The defining test is decision influence: in genuine co-design, something about the design is undecided when learners enter the room, and their contributions help decide it.

That test separates co-design from two look-alikes that consume most of the participation budget in real organizations:

- **Requirements-gathering** treats learners as information sources. The designer extracts needs, preferences, and pain points, then leaves to design alone. It is useful — Chapter 5 was largely requirements-gathering — but it positions the learner as a *subject of research*, not a *partner in design*. The power to frame the problem never changes hands.
- **Focus-group theater** is worse: participation staged after the decisions are made, to generate the appearance of consultation. Arnstein's (1969) classic ladder of citizen participation named this rung precisely — *tokenism* — and a half-century of participation research since has confirmed how common it is. If the redesign is already locked and the session exists so the deck can say "co-designed with learners," you are doing theater, and learners can usually tell.

The distinction matters because the two look-alikes are cheaper and safer, and because tokenistic participation is not neutral — it spends learner trust, which is finite. A session that solicits opinions and visibly ignores them teaches learners that their voice is decorative. You may only get to run that experiment once per cohort.

**Design application.** Before scheduling any session, write down: (a) which decisions are genuinely open, (b) which are closed and why, and (c) what learners will be told about both. If list (a) is empty, cancel the session and run interviews instead — that is the honest method for the situation you are actually in.

### The Participatory Design Lineage — and What the Evidence Actually Shows

Co-design did not begin in education. Its deepest root is the Scandinavian workplace-democracy tradition of the 1970s–80s, where researchers like Pelle Ehn worked with trade unions to give workers a say in the technologies restructuring their jobs (Ehn 1988). The founding claim was political before it was methodological: people affected by a designed system have a *right* to influence it. The methodological claim — that participation also produces *better* systems, because workers hold tacit knowledge designers lack — came alongside.

Two further branches matter here. In HCI, Druin (2002) developed cooperative inquiry, engaging children as full design partners rather than testers and showing that even young learners contribute design ideas adults systematically miss. In design research broadly, Sanders and Stappers (2008) mapped the shift from user-centered design (designing *for*) to co-design (designing *with*), arguing that the generative moment — where ideas come from — is where participation changes outcomes.

Within learning experience design specifically, the research base is younger and thinner than the method's popularity suggests. Recent work in the postdigital co-design literature reports that involving learners early changes not only artifact quality but the quality of the learning relationship itself — learners who co-design report greater ownership and engagement with the resulting experience [verify — this synthesis-level claim should be traced to the primary postdigital co-design studies before manuscript freeze]. CHI-adjacent work increasingly treats learner participation in design as a learning process in its own right, not just a requirements channel. And co-design with youth around AI tools has produced some of the field's most careful recent method work, including findings on how young partners negotiate privacy and relational boundaries with the systems they help design (Chang et al. 2025).

What the lineage does *not* provide is what this book keeps asking for: well-controlled evidence that co-designed learning experiences produce better *learning outcomes* than expert-designed ones. The honest summary is that co-design has strong ethical grounding, consistent qualitative evidence of better problem discovery and learner ownership, and almost no causal outcome evidence either way. The Evidence Box below carries the details. Hold that calibration: this chapter teaches co-design as a discovery and legitimacy method with documented relational benefits — not as a proven learning intervention.

**Design application.** Justify a co-design session by what it reliably delivers: access to experience knowledge you cannot get otherwise, early detection of mismatches between your design model and learners' lived reality, and a stronger relationship with the people your design serves. Do not justify it with outcome claims the literature cannot back.

### Structuring the Session: Roles, Artifacts, and the Feedback Matrix

Unstructured co-design collapses into either a complaint session or a referendum. Structure is what converts learner experience into design-usable material, and it has three load-bearing parts.

**Roles, declared up front.** Every participant should know what authority they hold in the room. A minimal frame: learners are *experience experts* (authoritative on what happened to them, what it felt like, what it costs them); the designer is the *integration authority* (responsible for synthesizing inputs against evidence and constraints); a facilitator — ideally not the designer whose work is under discussion — keeps the process honest. Stating roles aloud is not bureaucratic theater. It is what makes the authority boundary (next section) survivable, because the limits of learner decision power were established *before* any specific disagreement arose.

**Artifacts to think with.** People co-design better around things than around abstractions (Sanders & Stappers 2008). The strongest artifact for a learning experience session is the journey map from Chapter 6, printed large, because it lets learners annotate *their* experience onto *your* model of it — every divergence is a finding. Useful companions: experience cards (one moment per card, for sorting and ranking), rough sketches of candidate redesigns at deliberately low polish (polish suppresses critique — a Chapter 8 theme arriving early), and blank materials for learners to generate their own proposals.

**The feedback matrix.** Borrowed from service design practice (Stickdorn et al. 2018), the four-quadrant matrix — *what works / what should change / questions raised / new ideas* — does quiet but essential work: it separates evaluation from generation, forces negative feedback into actionable form, and gives every contribution a placed, visible home. Crucially, it also gives the designer an honest unit of record. After the session, every matrix item gets a disposition: **accepted** (and what changed), **rejected** (and on what grounds — evidence, constraint, or judgment), or **deferred** (awaiting what information). That accepted/rejected/why record is this week's studio deliverable, and it is the difference between co-design and theater made auditable.

**Design application.** Plan the session backwards from the record you must produce. If a planned activity cannot generate items you could disposition, cut it.

### The Authority-Boundary Problem

Now the hard part — the mechanism this chapter asks you to understand more deeply than any other.

Co-design distributes some design authority to learners. The authority-boundary problem is: *which* authority? The opening case is not an anomaly; it is the predictable collision of two facts.

**Fact one: learners hold real, exclusive expertise.** No amount of designer skill substitutes for knowing what the third week feels like on night shift, which explanation finally made sampling error click, or why the forum feels performative. On the *experience layer* — context, constraints, affect, meaning, cost — learner authority is close to absolute, because the designer literally has no independent access to the data.

**Fact two: learners' judgments of their own learning are systematically miscalibrated in a known direction.** This is not a vague claim about people being unreliable; it is one of the better-documented regularities in cognitive psychology. Learners judge learning by *current fluency* — how smooth and confident the experience feels right now — while durable learning is produced disproportionately by conditions that *reduce* current fluency. Soderstrom and Bjork (2015) frame this as the learning-versus-performance distinction: performance during instruction is visible and felt; learning is a latent change only measurable later, and the two routinely dissociate. The signatures are striking. In Kornell and Bjork's (2008) interleaving studies, most participants judged the less effective massed schedule the better one *even after* interleaving had just outperformed it on their own test. In Deslauriers et al. (2019), actively taught physics students learned measurably more than peers in fluent lectures — and reported *feeling* they learned less. And retrieval practice, the intervention in the opening case, is the canonical instance: it feels like testing rather than learning, is rated unpleasant, and beats restudy on delayed measures across a very large literature (Roediger & Karpicke 2006; Bjork & Bjork 2011).

So when learners ask for less desirable difficulty — fewer quizzes, blocked instead of interleaved practice, answers available sooner, smoother everything — they are not being lazy or foolish. They are accurately reporting fluency and reasonably (but wrongly) inferring learning from it. The miscalibration is the *expected output of a well-functioning metacognitive system pointed at the wrong signal.*

This yields the chapter's central tool, the **decision-rights split**:

| Layer | Examples | Authority | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Experience layer** | Timing, pacing, framing, tone, context fit, workload distribution, communication, what feels punitive or respectful | **Learner authority is presumptive.** Designer needs strong reasons to override. | Learners are the only source of this data; designer intuitions here are guesses. |
| **Learning-mechanism layer** | Retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, generation, feedback timing where evidence exists | **Evidence authority is presumptive.** Learner preference informs *implementation*, not *existence*. | Learner judgments here are systematically miscalibrated in a known direction. |
| **Ethics-and-constraints layer** | Privacy, assessment integrity, accessibility obligations, budget, policy | **Non-negotiable; disclosed, not voted.** | These bind the designer regardless of anyone's preference in the room. |

The split is not a license to ignore learners about quizzes. Look again at the opening case: almost everything the learners said was experience-layer gold. The quizzes feel like *surveillance*. They make a hard week *harder*. They arrive at a *bad time*. Those are design failures the designer can fix — stakes, framing, scheduling, feedback latency — without touching the mechanism. The evidence-disciplined response to "remove the quizzes" is almost never "no." It is: *"The retrieval practice stays, because the evidence for it is strong and your own report is the documented signature of it working. Everything about how it feels is now on the table — and you are the authority on that."*

One more obligation comes with the split: **say it out loud, and show your work.** A designer who silently overrules the room has converted co-design into theater retroactively. Disclose the boundary in the session brief; when you invoke it, name the evidence; record the overruled preference with the citation attached. "We heard you, here is what we changed, here is what we kept and the evidence why" builds trust rather than spending it.

### Ethics: Co-Design with Minors and Vulnerable Populations

Co-design asks more of participants than research does. An interviewee shares information; a design partner shares judgment, creative work, and often personal disclosure — and acquires a stake in decisions they may not ultimately control. That asymmetry creates ethical obligations beyond standard research ethics, and they sharpen when partners are minors, are institutionally subordinate to the designer (students co-designing the course that grades them), or belong to vulnerable groups.

Five obligations are minimal, not aspirational:

1. **Honest consent about influence.** Participants must know which decisions they can affect and which they cannot — *before* contributing. Overstating influence to improve recruitment is the original sin of tokenism.
2. **Role clarity and exit.** Partners may step back at any point without consequence — which has teeth only when the designer holds no grading or supervisory power, or has visibly firewalled it.
3. **Privacy as relational, not just informational.** Sessions surface peer-visible disclosures — struggle, failure, life constraints. Youth co-design work around AI tools shows young partners reasoning carefully about *relational* privacy: who, in the room and beyond, learns what about them (Chang et al. 2025). Ground rules about what leaves the room are method, not paperwork.
4. **Recognition and credit.** Design partnership is work. Name it, credit it, and where institutionally possible, compensate it.
5. **Extra structure for minors.** Children can be genuine design partners (Druin 2002), but only with adapted techniques, shorter cycles, guardian consent layered with the child's own assent, and facilitators alert to power gradients adults barely notice and children feel acutely.

For studio projects: if your Track B project involves minors or any population to whom you hold power, talk to your instructor *before* recruiting — IRB-equivalent guidance applies even to coursework, and the Track A panel is always the ethical default.

## The Evidence Box

**What is well-established:**

- **Learners misjudge conditions of learning, directionally.** Soderstrom & Bjork (2015), integrative review: judgments of learning track current performance and fluency, which dissociate from long-term retention. Kornell & Bjork (2008): most participants rated massed practice superior immediately after interleaving outperformed it in their own data. Deslauriers et al. (2019), N≈500 across course implementations: actively taught students learned more and reported feeling they learned less. Converging methods, replicated, direction consistent.
- **Retrieval practice improves delayed retention.** Roediger & Karpicke (2006) and a subsequent literature of hundreds of studies; among the most robust effects in learning science. Magnitudes vary with delay, feedback, and material — but the sign is stable. This is why the opening-case vote is predictably wrong.

**What is moderately supported:**

- **Co-design improves problem discovery and participant ownership.** Consistent qualitative and case-study evidence across PD's four decades (Ehn 1988; Druin 2002; Sanders & Stappers 2008) and in recent learning-technology co-design work (Chang et al. 2025). Mechanism is plausible (exclusive access to experience knowledge); outcome measurement is rare.

**What is thin or unsettled:**

- **Co-designed learning experiences → better learning outcomes.** Almost no controlled comparative evidence either way. The claim that co-design changes the learning relationship itself comes from a small postdigital co-design literature and should be treated as promising, not established [verify — single-cluster sourcing; trace to primary studies before citing in your own work].
- **Where the authority boundary should sit for *motivation* decisions.** Learner preference is miscalibrated for cognitive mechanisms, but autonomy support is itself an evidence-backed motivation mechanism (Chapter 4). When honoring a "wrong" preference would supply autonomy benefits, the trade has no settled answer. The field has not quantified this exchange rate.

**Single-source flags:** the postdigital relational-benefits claim (above); Chang et al. (2025) is one study population for the relational-privacy finding.

## Worked Example: A Co-Design Session for the Statistics Course

*Track A continuing case. Chapter 5 produced learner research on the asynchronous introductory statistics course (~140 enrolled; nursing, business, and psychology majors); Chapter 6 produced a journey map with a documented dropout cliff at Unit 4, Sampling Distributions — where the weekly graded retrieval quizzes begin and the simulation tool changes.*

**Situation.** The designer must set a redesign direction for Unit 4 before prototyping (Chapter 8). The journey map shows *where* the experience fails; it cannot show *why* from the inside. Seven learners are recruited: five currently enrolled, two who completed last term — including one who nearly dropped at Unit 4 and one who did drop and re-enrolled.

**The problem as the designer sees it.** Unit 4 stacks three frictions on one week: the first graded quiz, an unfamiliar simulation tool with no tutorial, and the course's hardest concept (sampling variability — where Chapter 5's research found the classic misconception that larger samples produce *more* variable sample means; cf. delMas et al. 2007). The designer suspects the quiz is taking blame for the stack. But that is a hypothesis held by the person who designed the quiz — exactly the situation co-design exists to check.

**Process — including the dead ends.** The designer plans a 90-minute session: roles declared (experience experts / integration authority / facilitator), decision brief stating openly that *whether retrieval practice exists* is evidence-bound and not on the table, while *everything about its implementation* is. Artifacts: the Unit 1–8 journey map printed at a meter wide, experience cards for Unit 4 moments, and a four-quadrant feedback matrix.

*Dead end one:* the first session plan opened with a brainstorm — "redesign Unit 4 however you like." Piloted with two colleagues, it produced feature wishlists unmoored from any experience data. Cut, replaced with annotate-the-journey-map first: ground in what happened before imagining what should.

*Dead end two:* mid-session, the facilitator slips and polls the room — "hands up if the quizzes should go." Six hands. The poll converts partners into a referendum bloc; the next ten minutes are advocacy, not design. The facilitator recovers by returning to the matrix: "That's recorded — six of seven want them gone. Now let's get specific about *what about them* fails you, because that part I can act on directly."

That move breaks the vote into experience-layer findings, and they are excellent: the quiz deadline (Sunday 23:59) lands on shift-change night for the nursing majors; feedback arrives five days later as a bare score with no explanations; the first attempt is graded cold, before any practice run; and the quiz banner ("Assessment 4.1") reads as judgment, not practice. One learner supplies the sentence the whole redesign will hang on: *"It's not that I mind being tested. I mind being graded on something I never got to try."*

**Resolution.** The accepted/rejected/why record, abridged:

- **Rejected:** removing weekly retrieval quizzes. Grounds: evidence authority (testing effect — Roediger & Karpicke 2006; learner removal preference is the predicted fluency illusion — Soderstrom & Bjork 2015). Stated to the panel in plain language, with the Kornell & Bjork result described; five of seven learners, told that the feeling of "it's not teaching me" is the documented signature of the mechanism working, accept the trade explicitly.
- **Accepted:** ungraded practice attempt before each graded quiz; immediate elaborated feedback (explanations, not bare scores); deadline moved off Sunday; quizzes reframed and renamed as practice checks with a two-sentence rationale note linking to the evidence ("why this course makes you retrieve").
- **Accepted:** a 10-minute sandbox tutorial for the simulation tool, decoupled from the graded week.
- **Deferred:** learner proposal for collaborative quiz attempts — genuinely interesting, no clear evidence either way; logged as an assumption for the Chapter 13 measurement plan.

**The lesson.** The vote to remove retrieval practice was wrong about the mechanism and right about nearly everything else — the designer's job was to split the two and honor each with its proper authority.

**The limit.** Seven self-selected learners cannot represent 140 — and the learners most harmed by Unit 4 are disproportionately the ones who already dropped and will never attend a session. Co-design also settles nothing empirical: whether the practice-attempt redesign actually moves completion or retention is an open question this record can only hand to Weeks 8 and 13. A co-design record is a direction with reasons — not a result.

## What Would Change My Mind

The decision-rights split rests on the claim that learners' in-the-moment preferences are systematically miscalibrated against delayed learning outcomes. A specific finding would force its revision: a well-powered study in authentic courses showing that honoring learner requests to remove desirable difficulties (retrieval practice, interleaving) produces *equal or better delayed retention* — for instance, because the autonomy and reduced anxiety of preference-honoring outweigh the lost difficulty benefits in real, motivation-fragile settings rather than laboratory ones. The components exist separately (autonomy benefits, difficulty benefits); a credible head-to-head in which the motivational channel wins would move the mechanism layer's presumption from "evidence overrules preference" toward "negotiate the trade case by case," and this chapter would need rewriting around that result.

## Still Puzzling

- **The exchange rate problem.** How much desirable difficulty is worth trading for how much autonomy-driven motivation? The two literatures barely speak to each other, and the design decision sits exactly at their junction.
- **Who co-designs for the departed?** The learners most damaged by a design are disproportionately the ones who dropped and will never attend a session. Trace data (Chapter 13) sees them; co-design cannot. Is there a participation method that reaches them, or is this a structural blind spot of the entire family of methods?
- **Does explaining the evidence change the preference?** Five of seven panel learners accepted retrieval practice once the fluency illusion was explained. Is informed preference durably recalibrated — would they choose quizzes next term? — or does the illusion reassert itself with the next felt difficulty? This seems testable and apparently untested [verify].

## Key Terms

- **Co-design (participatory design):** engaging the people affected by a design as partners in framing problems and generating solutions, with genuine influence over open decisions.
- **Requirements-gathering:** extracting needs and preferences from users as research input, with design authority retained entirely by the designer.
- **Tokenism (focus-group theater):** staged participation that creates the appearance of influence after decisions are made (Arnstein 1969).
- **Authority-boundary problem:** the question of which design decisions learner preference should govern, given that learners are authoritative about experience and systematically miscalibrated about learning.
- **Decision-rights split:** the three-layer allocation — experience layer (learner authority presumptive), learning-mechanism layer (evidence authority presumptive), ethics-and-constraints layer (disclosed, not voted).
- **Fluency illusion:** judging learning by current ease and smoothness, which dissociates from — and often inversely predicts — durable retention (Soderstrom & Bjork 2015).
- **Feedback matrix:** a four-quadrant session artifact (works / change / questions / ideas) that converts learner contributions into dispositionable design items.
- **Accepted/rejected/deferred record:** the post-session document assigning every contribution a disposition and grounds; the auditable trace that participation shaped decisions.

## Bridge

You now hold research, a journey map, and a co-design direction with its reasons on the record. Every one of those artifacts describes or prescribes — none of them *tests*. The redesigned Unit 4 exists only as intentions on a matrix, and intentions are cheap to hold and expensive to be wrong about. So the next move is the fast one: how do you make the smallest, roughest thing that can tell you whether the redesign teaches — before you spend a single week making it polished?

## Further Reading

- **Sanders, E. B.-N., & Stappers, P. J. (2008). "Co-creation and the new landscapes of design." *CoDesign*, 4(1).** The field's standard map of the shift from designing-for to designing-with, and where generativity enters.
- **Druin, A. (2002). "The role of children in the design of new technology." *Behaviour & Information Technology*, 21(1).** The canonical framework for children as design partners; the role taxonomy (user, tester, informant, partner) transfers far beyond childhood.
- **Soderstrom, N. C., & Bjork, R. A. (2015). "Learning versus performance: An integrative review." *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 10(2).** The mechanism behind the authority boundary, reviewed comprehensively; the single most important paper for defending an overrule.
- **Deslauriers, L., et al. (2019). "Measuring actual learning versus feeling of learning..." *PNAS*, 116(39).** The cleanest modern demonstration that felt learning and measured learning diverge in real classrooms — and a model of how to tell learners so.
- **Stickdorn, M., Hormess, M., Lawrence, A., & Schneider, J. (2018). *This Is Service Design Doing.* O'Reilly.** Chapters on co-creative workshop facilitation and feedback methods; the practical craft this chapter compressed.
