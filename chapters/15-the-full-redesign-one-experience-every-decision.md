## Opening Case: What Does Done Look Like?

*Sourced to this course: the instructor's Track A portfolio, built in segments across Chapters 12–14 and earlier studio weeks, presented here whole. Numbers are from the pilot term you evaluated in Chapter 14.*

On the last studio day, before any student presents, the instructor presents — and gets reviewed by the room under the same protocol the students will face. On screen: the complete redesign portfolio for the introductory-statistics online course. Fourteen weeks ago it was a course with 4.1/5 satisfaction, a 26% non-completion rate, and no evidence about learning either way. What "done" looks like is twelve artifacts and a ledger.

The artifacts, in argument order: the learner research that found low task-value, not low ability, at the center of the trouble (Week 5); the journey map locating the highest-risk moment at the sampling-distribution unit (Week 6); the co-design record, including the session where learners voted to remove the practice quizzes — and the memo explaining why the quizzes stayed (Week 7); the prototype and its test report (Week 8); the variability audit with one accessibility defect found, fixed, and disclosed (Week 9); the gamification memo that declined a leaderboard (Week 10); the modality memo that declined VR (Week 11); the AI decision — a hint ladder with a floor it cannot cross (Week 12); the measurement plan (Week 13); the evaluation, two registers, confound table and all (Week 14).

Then the part that makes first-time viewers uncomfortable: the **open decisions, shown rather than buried**. The progress map failed its novelty test — A3 refuted, replacement undecided. The 9-point gain is real but confounded; the decisive two-section comparison is *next* term, which means this portfolio's central learning claim is, in its own words, "not yet licensed." The retrieval-quiz spacing schedule is an educated guess awaiting local data. A student asks the obvious question: "You're presenting a redesign whose headline result you admit you can't claim yet. Why does this count as done?"

The instructor's answer is the chapter: **done, in evidence-disciplined design, does not mean every question is closed. It means every decision is accounted for** — grounded in evidence, grounded in learner research, or named as an assumption with its test specified and scheduled. A portfolio that claims more than that is not more finished; it is less honest. The ones that get funded twice, the instructor adds, are the honest ones — because the second ask arrives with the first ask's promises kept.

## Core Content

### 15.1 The Portfolio Is an Argument, Not a Gallery

The most common failure mode of design portfolios is the gallery: beautiful artifacts, arranged chronologically, each captioned with what it *is* rather than what it *did*. Galleries demonstrate effort. Hiring loops, budget meetings, and skeptical reviewers are not buying effort; they are buying judgment, and judgment is only visible as a chain of decisions under constraint.

So the unit of the portfolio is not the artifact. It is the **decision trace**: *what we believed → what evidence or research bore on it → what we decided → what we are still waiting to find out*. Each artifact appears as the evidence for a step in that trace. The journey map is not "a journey map"; it is *the reason the redesign concentrated on the sampling-distribution unit and not on the visual refresh the stakeholders originally requested*. McDonald and Westerberg's framing of LXD as an orienting guide — design moves justified by how people actually learn — describes exactly this genre: the portfolio is the argument that your design moves were so justified (McDonald & Westerberg 2023).

The required structure, both tracks:

1. **The brief** — the experience, its intended learning outcome, and its evidence of effectiveness at the start (usually: none; say so).
2. **The decision spine** — six to ten major decisions, each as a one-paragraph trace with pointers into the artifacts. Include at least one *declined* feature: the spine must show the word "no."
3. **The artifacts** — Weeks 5–14, each captioned by the decision it served.
4. **The ledger** — every assumption, with verdicts and scheduled tests for the open ones.
5. **The Evidence Disclosure, final form** (Section 15.2).
6. **Two registers of conclusion** — imported from Chapter 14 and updated.
7. **Track A only: the own-context section** — one decision spine segment transposed to a context you know personally (your discipline, your workplace), demonstrating the method travels with you and not just with the case.

**About holes.** A missing or weak artifact, disclosed — "co-design ran with two learners instead of five; the record is thin and the decisions it grounds are flagged accordingly" — costs points once. The same hole papered over costs the portfolio its credibility everywhere, because a reviewer who finds one silent gap stops believing the rest. Disclosure is not contrition; it is calibration, and calibration is the product.

### 15.2 The Evidence Disclosure at Full Strength

Every studio assignment this semester carried an Evidence Disclosure: which decisions are evidence-grounded, which research-grounded, which assumptions awaiting measurement. The final portfolio raises the bar, because by Week 15 you have something you did not have in Week 5 — a record of your own preferences colliding with evidence.

The **final Evidence Disclosure** must name, with artifact citations:

1. **Three decisions where evidence overruled preference** — yours, your learners', or your stakeholders'. Not three decisions where the evidence happened to agree with you; three where something you or they *wanted* lost. The disclosure must say who preferred what, which evidence overruled it, and what was decided instead.
2. **One decision where the evidence could not decide and judgment did.** State the competing options, why the evidence underdetermined the choice (absent, heterogeneous, or not transportable to your context), what you chose, on what grounds — and what future signal would tell you the judgment was wrong.

The asymmetry is deliberate. Anyone can narrate evidence agreeing with them. The capacity this course was built to produce shows up only in the other two moves: *losing to the evidence gracefully* and *choosing under acknowledged uncertainty without dressing the choice up as science*. The second move matters as much as the first — a portfolio claiming every decision was evidence-forced is either designing in a fantasy literature or laundering judgment as data, and your Week 9 work on contested UDL evidence already taught you which is more likely. Designing under honest uncertainty was named in Week 2 as the hardest move in the course; the final disclosure is where you prove you can make it on the record.

### 15.3 Peer Review: The Evidence Diagnostic, Aimed at a Colleague

Before any portfolio reaches the reviewer, it passes through a peer — and the peer review is itself graded work, because reviewing is a professional capability, not a courtesy.

The protocol runs the course's evidence diagnostic against six standard probes:

1. **Trace check.** Pick two decisions from the spine. Can you follow each from claim to artifact to evidence without the author in the room? A portfolio that needs its author is a talk, not a document.
2. **Register check.** Read the stakeholder conclusion, then the technical one. Does the stakeholder version claim anything the technical version cannot back? (You drilled this divergence in the Chapter 14 LLM exercise.)
3. **Causal-claim hunt.** Find every sentence implying *this design produced that outcome*. For each: is the claim licensed, confounded-and-qualified, or naked? Unsupported causal claims are the single most common portfolio defect — and the most expensive, because they are the easiest thing for a hostile reader to detonate.
4. **The declined-feature probe.** Find the "no." Is the case for the declined feature stated at its strongest — the steelman — before being declined? A "no" argued against a strawman is decoration, not discipline.
5. **Ledger audit.** Are the open assumptions genuinely open (test specified, scheduled), or are they euphemisms for "we hope"?
6. **The one-question rule.** End with the single question you would ask in the defense — the sharpest one you have. Giving a colleague your best question before the real defense is the entire point of peer review.

Two norms make this work. *Review the argument, not the person* — every probe is about traces and claims, none about talent. And *findings before the defense are gifts; the same findings during the defense are wounds*. Professional communities that institutionalize pre-review (code review, grand rounds, design critique) outperform those that rely on after-the-fact judgment; your studio has been practicing this all semester under the participation grade. This is the last rep with a net.

### 15.4 The Defense: Surviving the Skeptical Reviewer

The defense is fifteen minutes: five of presentation, ten of questioning by a reviewer whose role — announced in advance — is to be skeptical, informed, and fair. The format rewards exactly one thing: claims that match evidence. Three moves carry most defenses; their absence sinks most.

**Move one: concede fast and precisely.** When the reviewer lands a real hit — a confound you undersized, a trace that breaks — the winning response is immediate, specific concession with consequence: "Correct. That weakens the adoption recommendation from 'expand to all sections' to 'replicate first,' and it is already the ledger's next test." Defending the indefensible costs you every subsequent claim; precise concession buys credibility you will spend two questions later.

**Move two: distinguish the registers under pressure.** Reviewers will quote your stakeholder register and demand technical backing. If Chapter 14's discipline held, the backing exists line for line. If it didn't, the defense is where the seam shows — which is why the peer review's register check exists.

**Move three: defend the "no."** The format requires you to defend at least one decision to *decline* an engaging feature the evidence argued against — the leaderboard you didn't build, the VR you didn't buy, the AI affordance you fenced off, the practice testing you kept over your learners' objection. This is the course's thesis as a performance: the market selects for engagement; you must show you can select for learning *and justify it to people who wanted the engaging thing*. The defensible structure: steelman first ("here is the real appeal and the real evidence for it"), then the specific countervailing evidence (decay curves, the 17%-worse RCT, the touch-tank counterfinding, seductive details — whichever applies), then the criterion that decided ("durable learning for these learners, under this constraint"), then the door left open ("here is the data that would reverse this — and we are collecting it"). A "no" defended that way is not conservatism. It is the most senior-sounding thing a junior designer can say.

One more reviewer behavior to expect: the *invitation to overclaim*. "So the redesign improved learning by nine points?" The trap is that agreeing feels like success. The pass is: "Performance rose nine points; the term's confounds mean we can't yet attribute it; the replication that settles it runs next term." You will get this question. It is not a trick; it is the job.

### 15.5 Evidence-Disciplined Design as Professional Identity

The course ends, the mechanic doesn't. Outside this room there is no Evidence Disclosure rubric, no instructor checking your confound table's timestamp — and a market that, as Week 1 established, rewards engagement signals and rarely audits learning claims (Hirsh-Pasek et al., the EVER finding). Whether the discipline survives contact with that market is not a curriculum question. It is an identity question.

Be honest about what you are joining. LXD is an emerging field — borrowing from UX, ID, and the learning sciences without yet consolidating its own canon, by the bibliometric evidence (Jahnke et al.; the rebranding critique from Week 2 stands un-refuted) [contested — see pantry flag]. The labels on your future job postings will wobble: learning experience designer, learning engineer, instructional designer, product designer. What does not wobble is the capability split the market is already pricing: people who can make learning experiences people like are plentiful; people who can also *instrument them, evaluate them honestly, and defend a "no"* are not. The discipline is the differentiator precisely because the field hasn't standardized it.

What the identity consists of, stated as practice rather than virtue:

- **Every design decision is an empirical claim** — the book's thesis, now your default reflex. You ask "what would have to be true?" before "what would be cool?", and you noticed weeks ago that you can't stop.
- **The ledger travels with you.** Real products ship with assumptions; professionals ship with *named* assumptions and scheduled tests. The deliverable is never just the design — it is the design plus the honest map of its uncertainty.
- **You are the person in the room who asks the measurement question** — kindly, early, with a cheap test attached. Not the cynic; the one who makes claims keepable.
- **You lose to evidence in public and on purpose**, because you have learned that the alternative is losing to reality later, with interest, in front of learners.

And the obligation underneath: learners trust that experiences labeled educational teach. The market does not enforce that promise — Week 1's five-star apps, Week 12's well-liked tutor that taught 17% less. You are the enforcement. That is not a burden the field hands you with a credential; it is one you pick up, portfolio by portfolio, "no" by defensible "no."

## Worked Example: The Portfolio, Whole

*Act Three continuing case, final segment — the instructor's complete Track A portfolio. This worked example serves both tracks by design: Track A students submit this case's structure with their own own-context section; Track B students mirror the structure on their own project.*

**Situation.** Fourteen weeks of segments — research, map, co-design record, prototype, audit, three decision memos, measurement plan, evaluation — and a chair still waiting on a recommendation. The remaining work was not production but *assembly into an argument*, which turned out to be neither.

**The problem as the designer sees it.** Chronological assembly produced a diary, not a case. Worse, re-reading old artifacts with Week-14 eyes surfaced embarrassments: a causal claim in the Week 8 test report ("the simulation improved understanding") that no evidence then licensed; a Week 5 persona trait ("statistics anxiety") that quietly drove two design decisions despite resting on three interviews.

**Process, including dead ends.** *Dead end one: polish the diary.* The chronological draft with better captions still made the reviewer do all the work of finding the argument. Killed against the trace check (15.3, probe 1). *Dead end two: erase the embarrassments.* The instinct was to silently rewrite the Week 8 overclaim. The discipline said otherwise: the portfolio keeps the original line, struck through, with the Week 14 correction beside it — turning a defect into displayed calibration. (A peer reviewer later called this the most convincing page in the document.) *The turn:* rebuild around a seven-decision spine — (1) target the sampling-distribution unit, not a visual refresh; (2) simulation with prediction-first interaction; (3) keep retrieval quizzes over the co-design vote; (4) decline the leaderboard; (5) decline VR; (6) AI hint ladder with a floor; (7) adopt-in-one-section, replicate before scaling — each with its trace, each pointing into the artifacts.

**Resolution.** The portfolio as filed: brief; seven-trace spine; artifacts captioned by the decisions they served; the ledger (A2, A4 survived; A3 refuted — progress-map replacement *undecided and stated as such*; A1 partially supported; A5 open pending the two-section comparison); the two registers from Chapter 14, unchanged because they were written to survive; and the final Evidence Disclosure. The three evidence-over-preference entries: the leaderboard (stakeholders and, candidly, the instructor wanted it; extrinsic-decay and crowding evidence overruled — Zeng et al. 2024 heterogeneity, SDT alignment); VR for sampling visualizations (the instructor's enthusiasm; overruled by extraneous-load and novelty mechanisms, the touch-tank counterfinding, and cost against a browser simulation testing equal on the learning task); removing practice quizzes (the learners' unanimous co-design preference; overruled by the retrieval-practice literature — with the compromise honestly recorded: quizzes stayed, stakes dropped to near-zero, framing rewritten from "quiz" to "checkpoint"). The one judgment call: a weekly synchronous "data clinic." The sync/async evidence shows small, inconsistent differences — it could not decide. Judgment chose the clinic on relatedness grounds (Chapter 4) and the journey map's isolation signal at week three; the named reversal signal is attendance collapse plus no movement in belonging self-reports by week six. **Defense excerpt, the declined leaderboard:** *"Steelman: leaderboards reliably spike short-run behavioral engagement, and our completion problem is real. But the spike decays after novelty, can crowd out the intrinsic motivation we measured as already fragile, and pulls design attention to the metric, not the learning. Our criterion is durable learning for low-task-value general-education students. The progress map we tried instead just failed its novelty test too — that's in the ledger, openly. The difference is the map cost us two design days, and the leaderboard would have cost a semester of motivational signal we can't get back. If next term's data shows sustained-engagement collapse without a competitive mechanic, decision 4 reopens — that test is scheduled."*

**The lesson (one sentence).** A finished portfolio is not a record of being right; it is a traceable record of deciding well — including the corrections, the refusals, and the questions still open on the day it shipped.

**The limit (where this approach fails).** This portfolio's honesty is load-bearing only where reviewers reward calibration; in venues that read qualifiers as weakness (some procurement processes, some interview loops), the two-register discipline helps but does not guarantee the honest portfolio beats the confident gallery — a market condition you now know how to name, and which Week 1 predicted.

**Track A and Track B.** Track A: submit the instructor's case rebuilt in your own argument — your spine, your captions, your disclosure — plus the required own-context section transposing one decision trace to a context you know. (Identical spines with identical reasoning are a red flag; the case is shared, the judgment must be yours.) Track B: your project, complete, same structure; your pilot data (or its honest absence, with the scheduled test) in the ledger.

## Evidence Disclosure (final, higher-bar version)

> **Decision census:** Every major decision in the spine, labeled: *evidence-grounded* (cite it), *research-grounded* (name the artifact and week), or *assumption awaiting measurement* (name the scheduled test).
> **Three overrulings:** Three decisions where evidence overruled preference — who preferred what, the evidence that overruled, the decision taken. At least one must involve declining or constraining an engaging feature.
> **One judgment call:** The decision the evidence could not settle — the options, why the evidence underdetermined them, the grounds for the choice, and the future signal that would reverse it.
> **The corrections:** Any claim made earlier in the semester that this portfolio retracts or weakens, shown rather than erased.
> **The open ledger:** Every assumption still open on submission day, each with its test and date. An empty open-ledger section will be read as overclaiming, not completeness.

## What Would Change My Mind

The defense format and the final disclosure presume that calibrated, qualifier-bearing portfolios outperform confident galleries where it matters — hiring loops, budget meetings, scaling decisions. That is itself a partly untested design decision. Systematic evidence that evaluators in real LXD hiring and procurement consistently *penalize* disclosed uncertainty — that the confident gallery reliably wins, and that the penalty persists after evaluators are trained on calibration — would force a redesign: not of the underlying discipline (the epistemics don't change), but of this chapter's communication strategy, toward something like a layered portfolio in which calibration is fully present but progressively disclosed. I would regret that revision, and I would make it, which is the posture this book has been recommending all along.

## Still Puzzling

These are the book's open questions as much as the chapter's; you inherit them.

1. **Durability.** Almost everything in the evidence spine — gamification, immersion, AI tutoring, your own pilot — is measured over weeks or a semester. Whether evidence-disciplined redesigns produce *durable* advantages over years is largely unknown, because the longitudinal studies mostly do not exist yet.
2. **Measuring the distinction that matters.** The book's central pair — behavioral versus cognitive engagement — still lacks a standardized measurement framework. Until one exists, every measurement plan in this course is a local, hand-validated solution to a problem the field has not solved in general [contested — see pantry flag].
3. **The AI ground shifting underfoot.** The crutch-effect mechanism looks stable; the tools, the usage patterns, and the thin causal base (20 studies in 800+ papers) are not. The guardrail logic you learned may outlive every specific finding you cited. Will it?
4. **Whether the field consolidates.** If LXD standardizes its methods and outcome measures, this book's discipline becomes the field's floor. If it remains a borrowing field, the discipline remains a personal practice that you carry between rooms that don't require it. Both futures are live; your career will be data.

## Key Terms

- **Decision trace:** the unit of portfolio argument — belief, evidence bearing on it, decision, what remains open.
- **Decision spine:** the portfolio's six to ten major traces, including at least one declined feature.
- **Final Evidence Disclosure:** the higher-bar disclosure — three evidence-over-preference decisions plus one judgment call under undecidable evidence.
- **Steelman:** the strongest honest version of the position you are about to argue against; mandatory before any declined-feature defense.
- **Open ledger:** the portfolio's list of live assumptions, each with a scheduled test; the signature of calibration rather than incompleteness.
- **Defense format:** five minutes of claims, ten of skeptical questioning; rewards concession precision and register consistency.
- **Calibration:** the match between confidence expressed and evidence held — the portfolio's actual product.
- **Evidence-disciplined design:** the professional identity in which every design decision is treated as an empirical claim that must survive the evidence.

## Closing the Book: The Trap, Revisited

There is no next chapter, so the bridge runs backward. In Week 1 you met a five-star app — millions of downloads, glowing reviews, minimal learning value by evidence-based criteria — and the question that started everything: *what exactly did the five stars measure?*

You can answer it now, at every level the book has built. The five stars measured affective reaction — real, and not learning (Chapter 14). The streak counter behind them measured behavioral engagement — presence, not processing (Chapters 1, 13). The smoothness that earned the reviews likely came from stripping the difficulties that make learning durable (Chapter 3), and the delight may have been seductive detail doing its quiet damage (Chapter 1). The app never ran a delayed, unassisted, aligned performance task, so on the only question that justifies the word "educational," its dashboard had — say it with precision now — *no vocabulary*. And the market that made it a bestseller was selecting on exactly the signals it did measure (Week 1; Week 15, still true).

In Week 1, the trap needed a chapter to explain. It just took you a paragraph to diagnose — and you could write the measurement plan that would catch it, the evaluation that would say so in two registers, and the defense of the redesign that would fix it, including the engaging features you would decline. The trap is still out there; it is fully funded and shipping weekly. What changed is you. The market selects for engagement. You select for learning — and now you can prove the difference.

## LLM Exercise: Red-Team Your Own Portfolio

*Productive-struggle guardrail: this is the final LLM exercise, run under the strictest rule of the semester — the model attacks your finished work; it writes none of it. Paste only your own completed draft. The model may not draft concessions, rewrite disclosures, or supply evidence labels; everything it surfaces is input to revisions you write yourself. Run it after peer review and before the human defense — in that order, so a human's judgment frames the machine's, not the reverse. The deliverable is your defense-preparation memo, not the transcript.*

Paste your decision spine, final Evidence Disclosure, and both conclusion registers, then:

> You are the skeptical reviewer at my final portfolio defense — informed, fair, and unimpressed by polish. Attack in this order, and after each phase ask your questions and STOP for my typed answers before continuing. **Phase 1 — causal claims:** quote every sentence that implies my design caused an outcome and rank the three least defensible. Ask me to defend the weakest, then critique my defense for precision of concession. **Phase 2 — the disclosure:** test my three evidence-over-preference decisions: for each, am I describing evidence genuinely overruling a preference, or evidence conveniently agreeing with me? Test my one judgment call: did I launder judgment as evidence anywhere? **Phase 3 — the declined feature:** argue FOR the engaging feature I declined, at full strength, citing its best evidence. Push back on my rebuttal twice. **Phase 4 — the verdict I'm avoiding:** name the single question from this transcript I answered worst. Do not suggest improved answers at any phase — that is my work, not yours.

Deliverable: a one-page defense-preparation memo — the two weakest points the red-team exposed, your revised (self-written) responses, one place the model's attack was wrong and the evidence that shows it, and the question you most hope the human reviewer doesn't ask, with the honest answer you will give if they do.
