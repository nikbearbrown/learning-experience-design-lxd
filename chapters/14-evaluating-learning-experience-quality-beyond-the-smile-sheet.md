## Opening Case: The 71-Point NPS

*Illustrative case — a composite built from the documented gap between satisfaction metrics and learning outcomes (Alliger et al. 1997; Reichheld 2003; EDUCAUSE 2025); organization and numbers invented.*

The learning team at a logistics company built "Forward," a six-week onboarding academy, and by every number leadership has seen, it is the most successful thing the team has shipped. Net Promoter Score: 71 — territory occupied by beloved consumer brands. Completion: 96%. The open comments read like fan mail. The CLO has drafted the expansion announcement: Forward goes global next quarter, budget tripled. The draft is in your inbox for "a quick data sanity check by Friday."

You are the analyst, and you have the rest of the data. Ninety-day performance metrics for Forward graduates — error rates, time-to-independence, supervisor ratings — are statistically indistinguishable from the cohort onboarded under the old program. The delayed knowledge check, administered quietly at week eight, shows the same: no detectable difference. You also know what the 71 measures, because Chapter 1 taught you: NPS is a loyalty and sentiment instrument (Reichheld 2003). It asks whether people would *recommend* the experience. It is real data about a real thing — and the thing is not learning. The classic meta-analytic finding is blunt here: reactions to training correlate weakly, near zero by some estimates, with how much was actually learned (Alliger et al. 1997) [verify — confirm reported coefficients against the primary paper].

Here is what makes this case hard, and why it opens this chapter rather than a chapter on statistics. If you write "no evidence of learning impact," you will be technically correct and organizationally dead: the announcement is drafted, the success story has momentum, and your memo reads as an attack on a beloved program. If you sign off, you convert a sentiment score into a learning claim with your name on it — and when the performance gap surfaces in a year, the program, the team, and the measurement function all lose credibility at once.

The way out is not diplomacy or capitulation. It is an evaluation genre most analysts are never taught: the **qualified conclusion in two registers** — one that survives a technical review, one that survives a leadership meeting, both saying the same true thing. Writing it is this chapter's terminal skill. The truth, told so it can be used: Forward is a genuinely excellent *experience* whose *learning* effect is currently unknown — and here is the cheap, fast test that would settle it before tripling the budget.

## Core Content

### 14.1 What the Smile Sheet Actually Measures

The end-of-course satisfaction survey — the "smile sheet" — is the most administered evaluation instrument in education and training, and the most misread. The misreading is not that smile sheets are worthless. It is that they are treated as evidence for a claim they do not address.

The Kirkpatrick framework, the field's default since the late 1950s, names four levels: reaction, learning, behavior, results (Kirkpatrick 1959; Kirkpatrick and Kirkpatrick 2016). The smile sheet is Level 1. The empirical problem, established for decades, is that Level 1 does not predict Level 2: the Alliger et al. (1997) meta-analysis found reaction measures correlate only weakly with learning measures — and Chapter 1 gave you the mechanism, not just the correlation. Desirable difficulties make effective experiences feel worse in the moment; seductive details make ineffective ones feel better (Bjork's desirable-difficulties program; Rey 2012). Satisfaction and learning are not two readings of one dial. They are different dials, sometimes wired in opposition.

So the evidence-disciplined position has two clauses, and you need both. *Clause one:* reaction data is real data — about attrition risk, word-of-mouth, friction, affect, and the motivational prerequisite (Chapter 4) without which learners disengage entirely. Will Thalheimer's reconstruction of the smile sheet shows how to make reaction data more decision-useful — ask about specific design elements, perceived ability to apply, and barriers, rather than global delight (Thalheimer 2016). *Clause two:* no quantity of reaction data, however well designed, sums to a learning claim. A 4.8/5 course rating and a 71 NPS answer the question "did they like it?" completely, and the question "did it teach?" not at all.

**Design application:** keep the smile sheet; demote it. In your evaluation it occupies one named row — *experience indicators* — never the headline.

### 14.2 Mixed-Model Evaluation: The Scorecard Class

If satisfaction alone is insufficient, what does a complete evaluation contain? The field's working answer is **mixed-model evaluation**: experience indicators, participation metrics, and learning outcomes, read together, none substituting for another. The institutional embodiment is the quality-scorecard class of instruments, of which the Online Learning Consortium (OLC) scorecards are the most widely adopted exemplar: structured rubrics covering course design, engagement design, assessment alignment, and accessibility (OLC Quality Scorecard suite).

Understand precisely what a scorecard is, because misuse runs in both directions. A scorecard audits whether the experience is *built according to the current consensus on good design* — outcomes stated and aligned, interaction designed rather than accidental, assessment matched to objectives, accessibility obligations met. That is process evidence. It catches real, expensive defects early: the course whose final exam tests content no activity taught; the "discussion-based" course with no designed reason to discuss; the video library no screen reader can traverse. An OLC-class review before launch is among the cheapest quality moves available — it is Chapter 9's variability audit and Chapter 6's journey logic institutionalized as a checklist.

But a scorecard score is not an outcome. A course can satisfy every rubric line and still not produce learning in your context, for your learners, this term — and a course can score imperfectly and work. (The mechanism is no mystery: rubric lines encode design consensus, and this book has spent fourteen weeks showing that consensus design choices carry heterogeneous effects.) The scorecard's epistemic ceiling is: *no known design defects of the kinds the rubric can see.*

The three-layer mixed model, then — and the reading discipline that goes with it:

| Layer | Instruments | Can claim | Cannot claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| Design quality | OLC-class scorecard, accessibility audit | built to consensus standards | that it taught anyone |
| Experience & participation | smile sheets (Thalheimer-style), NPS, telemetry from your Ch. 13 plan | learners showed up, persisted, felt X; engagement patterns Y | that engagement was learning |
| Learning outcomes | delayed aligned performance, transfer tasks, embedded common items | what changed in capability, with stated uncertainty | *why* it changed (without design evidence and confound analysis) |

**Design application:** structure every evaluation report in these three named layers. The discipline is in the joints — each layer answers its own question, and the report never lets a lower layer answer a higher layer's question.

### 14.3 The ROI Gap: What Organizations Say, What They Measure

You will run your evaluations inside organizations, and organizations are systematically strange about evaluation. The EDUCAUSE 2025 ROI QuickPoll puts numbers on the strangeness: **71% of higher-education technology leaders rate measuring ROI on academic technology as important; only 46% actually measure it** — and most of those who do reduce "return" to hours saved or costs avoided rather than anything about learning (EDUCAUSE 2025).

Read that gap as a designer, because it is a design environment, not a scandal. Three forces produce it. First, *outcome measurement looks expensive and contestable* while hours-saved looks cheap and objective — so the available metric displaces the relevant one (the McNamara fallacy, and Goodhart's cousin from Chapter 13). Second, *nobody owns the question*: the learning team owns delivery, finance owns cost, no one owns "did capability change?" Third, *the deferral instinct*: Hubbard's observation that organizations treat "hard to measure" as "impossible to measure," when even a small, ugly, fast measurement usually beats the alternative — which is deciding blind (Hubbard 2010).

The trap for your career is inheriting the hours-saved frame. An evaluation that reports "the new course saves 90 minutes per learner" has said nothing about learning — a course of empty pages would save even more time. (Joel Best's rule travels well here: every statistic is produced by someone, for some purpose, and "hours saved" is a number produced to be easy, not to be true about learning; Best 2001.) The counter-move is not to refuse the ROI conversation but to *re-anchor it*: pair every efficiency number with a capability number from layer three of your mixed model, and when no capability number exists, say so in writing — "time savings confirmed; learning impact unmeasured; here is the cheap test." That sentence, written before the expansion announcement, is the entire professional value of an evaluator.

**Design application:** in any stakeholder-facing evaluation, the cost/efficiency claim and the learning claim appear in the same table, each with its own evidence status. Never let one borrow the other's credibility.

### 14.4 Confound Honesty

Your pilot produced numbers. Before any conclusion, you owe the reader — and yourself — the rival explanations. A **confound** is anything other than your design that could have produced the observed difference. Real evaluations of real pilots are confounded almost by definition; the discipline is not eliminating confounds (you usually can't) but *naming them, sizing them, and stating what would discriminate*. Spiegelhalter's framing is the right posture: statistical claims earn trust by being transparent about what they cannot rule out (Spiegelhalter 2019).

The recurring cast, in roughly the order they will visit your pilot:

- **Selection.** Pilot sections are rarely random — volunteers, one instructor's section, a motivated cohort. If your pilot learners differ from baseline learners, the difference travels into your results.
- **History / co-intervention.** The term you piloted, other things changed too: a new advisor, a schedule shift, the LMS upgrade.
- **Novelty and enthusiasm effects.** New things get extra attention from learners *and from you*. Chapter 11's XR literature showed novelty inflating early results; your redesign is not exempt. The instructor teaching their own redesign teaches harder.
- **Instrumentation shift.** If the measure changed with the design (new quiz, new rubric), the measure is a rival explanation for the change.
- **Regression to the mean.** Redesigns are triggered by bad terms; bad terms are often followed by better ones for no reason but statistical gravity (Spiegelhalter 2019).
- **Item exposure / teaching to the measure.** Common items reused across terms can leak; gains on familiar items are ambiguous between learning and exposure.

Two practices convert this list from ritual to method. First, the **confound table**: each rival explanation gets a row — *plausible size* (could it account for the whole effect, part, none?), *evidence bearing on it* (what you already logged — your Chapter 13 telemetry frequently does this work), and *what would discriminate* (the next test). Second, **write the confound table before unblinding yourself to the headline result** where possible. Confounds enumerated after you know you "won" have a way of shrinking; Best documents at length how numbers get massaged toward the conclusion their producers need (Best 2001). The order of operations is itself an integrity instrument.

**Design application:** no evaluation leaves your desk without a confound table. Its absence is not a style choice; it is an overclaim in progress.

### 14.5 The Two-Register Conclusion

The 71-NPS case fails analysts in two opposite ways: the technically perfect memo nobody can use, and the usable memo that is not true. The two-register conclusion is the genre that refuses both failures. One finding, two documents, zero contradiction.

**The technical register** is written for someone who will check you: a methodologist, a future evaluator, you-in-two-years. Its rules: every claim carries its evidence and its uncertainty; effect sizes with intervals where you have them, raw differences honestly labeled where you don't; the confound table is in the body, not an appendix; the assumption ledger is updated line by line (tested/survived/open); and the conclusion is allowed to be conditional, because conditional is what the data licenses.

**The stakeholder register** is written for someone who must decide and will not read past the first page. Its rules are stricter, not looser, because compression is where overclaiming breeds:

1. **Lead with the decision recommendation,** not the methods.
2. **One sentence of grounds, in plain language.** No coefficients; "stronger/weaker than," "we can't yet tell apart," "about X points."
3. **The qualifier is load-bearing and concrete.** Not "more research is needed" (a phrase that means nothing and is read as a throat-clear) but *what specifically is unknown and what it would cost to know*: "We don't yet know if the gain lasts; a 20-minute follow-up quiz in March answers this for free."
4. **Never trade a true qualified claim for a clean false one** — and never bury the true claim so deep in hedges that the reader supplies their own overclaim. Spiegelhalter's communication research is encouraging on exactly this point: audiences tolerate honestly communicated uncertainty better than experts fear (Spiegelhalter 2019).

The hardest stakeholder sentence — practice it until it stops feeling like failure — is the honest non-result: **"This evaluation is not definitive, and here is what would settle it."** Said with a price tag and a date, that sentence is not weakness; it is the only thing in the meeting that compounds. The Forward memo that survives is: *"Forward is the best-received onboarding we have ever run — and its effect on job capability is currently unknown. Recommend: hold global expansion one quarter; run the 90-day comparison on the next two cohorts (cost: ~zero, the data already exists); expand with evidence or fix with evidence."* Truth, with a handle on it.

**Design application:** write the technical register first, always. The stakeholder register is a compression of a document that exists, never a separate, friendlier truth.

## Worked Example: Evaluating the Statistics Pilot

*Act Three continuing case, segment four. The Chapter 13 measurement plan met one pilot term of data. Track A students receive this dataset; what follows is the instructor's own evaluation of it.*

**Situation.** Pilot section, n = 61, ran the redesigned sampling-distribution unit with the full instrumentation plan. Baseline: two prior terms (n = 263) of the unredesigned course, same instructor, with six common final-exam items and standard LMS records.

**The problem as the designer sees it.** Five open assumptions (A1–A5) await verdicts. The department chair has asked one question: "Should the other three sections adopt the redesign next term?" That question has an honest answer and it is not yes or no.

**Process, including dead ends.** *Dead end one: the victory memo.* The first draft led with the headline — common-item performance up 9 percentage points; satisfaction 4.5 vs 4.1; completion 81% vs 74% — and recommended adoption. It died on the instructor's own confound table, written (a term too late, and noted as such in the portfolio) *after* the headline was known: selection (pilot enrolled later in registration — different student mix, plausibly accounts for part), novelty-plus-enthusiasm (instructor's own energy; plausibly part), item exposure (the six common items were in three terms of circulation; the telemetry showed two of six with anomalously fast response times — plausibly part), regression (baseline included the course's worst term on record). The 9 points could be all design, and could be mostly artifact; the data cannot tell these apart. *Dead end two: the penance memo.* The second draft hedged everything into uselessness — fourteen qualifiers, no recommendation. The chair's question deserved an answer; "it's complicated" is not one.

**Resolution.** The mixed model, then two registers. **Layer 1 (design):** OLC-class self-review passed except one accessibility defect (simulation not fully keyboard-navigable — fix scheduled, disclosed). **Layer 2 (experience/participation):** satisfaction up; telemetry verdicts on the assumption ledger — A2 *survived* (74% predict-before-run; spam cluster 12%, below threshold); A4 *survived with a flag* (66% attempt-before-hint — above the 60% threshold — but hint-exhaustion 11% concentrated in the lowest-prior-knowledge quintile, echoing the dependency-equity warning from Chapter 12); A3 *failed* (progress-map return visits collapsed after week 6 — novelty decay, exactly as the Chapter 10 evidence predicted for map-only mechanics); A1 *partially tested* (task-value self-reports rose 0.6 on a 5-point scale unit-to-unit; self-report, vulnerable to demand effects). **Layer 3 (learning):** common items +9 points (confounded as above); the 3-week delayed quiz on new isomorphic items — the one instrument with no historical counterpart — showed 68% mastery-level performance, which is *encouraging and uninterpretable against no baseline*, now logged as the baseline for next term. A5: *open*.

The two registers, as filed. *Technical:* "Participation and experience targets met; A2 and A4 supported by telemetry; A3 refuted; learning gains positive but confounded by selection, exposure, novelty, and regression; delayed-retention baseline established; causal claim not licensed." *Stakeholder (the chair's answer, in full):* "Recommend adopting in one additional section, not all three. The redesign is clearly liked and clearly used as designed, and students score higher — but this term cannot distinguish 'the redesign works' from 'pilot enthusiasm and a weak baseline.' Two sections next term — one redesigned, one not, same items, fresh items — settles it by June at no extra cost. If the gain holds, adopt everywhere with evidence; if not, we will know which parts to keep, because the telemetry tells us what students actually used."

**The lesson (one sentence).** An evaluation's job is not to declare victory or confess failure but to convert one term's ambiguous data into next term's decisive test — and to say so in words a decision-maker can act on.

**The limit (where this approach fails).** Two-register honesty assumes a stakeholder who, given a truthful qualified answer and a cheap decisive test, will take the test; in organizations that punish qualified answers, the genre protects the record but not necessarily the evaluator — a limit Chapter 15 picks up as a matter of professional identity.

## What Would Change My Mind

This chapter treats satisfaction data as categorically insufficient evidence for learning claims. A large, replicated body of work showing that *well-designed* reaction instruments (Thalheimer-class, asking about specific design elements and perceived applicability rather than global delight) predict delayed learning outcomes at practically useful strength — say, correlations consistently above r ≈ .4 across domains and delays — would force a real revision: smile sheets would graduate from "experience indicator" to "weak learning proxy," and the economics of evaluation would change, because reaction data is nearly free. The current evidence (Alliger et al. 1997 and successors) sits far below that bar, but the bar is statable, and that is the point of stating it.

## Still Puzzling

1. **The incentive asymmetry.** The honest evaluator's qualified conclusion competes with the vendor's clean overclaim, and the overclaim often wins the meeting. Is two-register honesty a sustainable individual practice, or does it require organizational protection (evaluation charters, pre-registration norms) to survive contact with real budget cycles?
2. **How cheap can a decisive test get?** The chapter leans on "here is the cheap test that would settle it." For some questions (durable transfer, long-run equity effects) no cheap test exists. What does the stakeholder register honestly say when the decisive test costs more than the decision?
3. **Evaluating the evaluator.** Confound tables written after seeing results shrink; we ask students to disclose timing. But the field has no standard practice for auditing evaluation integrity in industry LXD work. What would a "pre-registered pilot evaluation" norm look like for design teams, and would anyone adopt it?

## Key Terms

- **Smile sheet:** end-of-experience reaction survey; Level 1 of the Kirkpatrick framework; evidence about experience, not learning.
- **Kirkpatrick levels:** reaction, learning, behavior, results — useful as a vocabulary for *which question* an instrument addresses.
- **NPS (Net Promoter Score):** a loyalty/sentiment metric; in learning contexts, an experience indicator only.
- **Mixed-model evaluation:** evaluation combining design-quality review, experience/participation metrics, and learning outcomes, with no layer substituting for another.
- **Quality scorecard (OLC-class):** a structured rubric auditing design, engagement, assessment, and accessibility; process evidence with a known ceiling.
- **Confound:** any rival explanation for an observed difference; named and sized in a confound table.
- **Regression to the mean:** the statistical tendency of extreme results to be followed by less extreme ones, redesign or no redesign.
- **Two-register conclusion:** one finding stated twice — for technical checkability and for stakeholder decision-making — with no contradiction between them.
- **Hours-saved ROI:** the reduction of learning value to time efficiency; legitimate as a cost claim, empty as a learning claim.

## LLM Exercise: The Two Hostile Readers

*Productive-struggle guardrail: this exercise requires your completed Studio Assignment #9 — your own evaluation, both registers, confound table included. The model may interrogate your artifact; it may not draft conclusions, soften qualifiers, or invent evidence. You write every revision.
