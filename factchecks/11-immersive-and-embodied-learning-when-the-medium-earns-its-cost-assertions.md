- **No "students with prior XR experience scored better" moderator** is reported.

**Expert review needed:** YES — high priority. The opening scene, the 31.1% statistic, the "second curriculum" mechanism reveal, the prior-experience "validating detail," and the pre-training citation all rest on this paper and are not supported by it. Options: (a) rewrite the anecdote to match the real study (which still teaches "medium must repay its cost," but via "combination wins / VR alone underperforms hands-on"), or (b) find a different study that actually reports a head-to-head deficit with a cognitive-load mechanism (such studies exist — e.g., immersive-VR-vs-lower-immersion comparisons showing novelty/extraneous-load effects — and should be substituted and re-cited).
**Suggested reference (for the real study, used honestly):** Walters, L. J., et al. (2026). *Frontiers in Education*, 11, 1774496. doi:10.3389/feduc.2026.1774496 — to be cited for "both methods add value; combining 360° video with hands-on is most effective," not for a 31.1% hands-on victory.

## Resolution (2026-06-07)

All corrections applied. In the course of applying them, the full text of Walters et al. (2026, *Frontiers in Education*, 11:1774496) was re-fetched and read in full — and the re-verification found that **this report's own first finding was partially in error**. The record, corrected:

**What the paper actually contains (contrary to the finding above):**
- The **31.1% figure is in the paper**, verbatim, in the Discussion: "learning assessment scores were 31.1% higher after the hands-on lesson compared to scores from the 360° video experience."
- The **prior-XR-experience moderator is in the paper** (Results §3.2): "previous XR usage was correlated with better assessment scores, where XR use on at least a monthly basis was associated with higher scores compared to students that had never used XR before this study (z = 2.3, p = 0.02)."
- The **novelty-effect interpretation is in the paper** (Discussion, citing Lee et al. 2025), and a **pre-training suggestion is in the paper**: "Pre-training in XR applications … may help reduce this novelty effect and improve learning outcomes (Howard and Lee, 2020)."
- **Touch tanks are genuinely part of the hands-on lesson** (oyster touch tanks; students found crabs in the clusters).
- Measured means: pre-test 43.2%, 360°-video-only 62.1% (n = 38), hands-on-only 83.6% (n = 41), combined 79.6%; hands-on significantly above 360° video (p < 0.0001); combined ≈ hands-on (p = 0.54).

**What was genuinely wrong in the chapter (and is now fixed):**
1. The medium was a filmed **360° video in headsets**, not a "360° VR rendering"; the chapter's opener now says so.
2. The hands-on animal roster ("sea stars, urchins, anemones," hermit crab, docent) was invented; replaced with the paper's actual activities (oyster touch tanks, crabs, filtration demo, restoration materials).
3. "The study's mechanism analysis explains exactly why" overstated the paper: there is no formal mechanism analysis. The paper's own reading is passive-vs-active media plus the novelty effect; the cognitive-load framing is the chapter's, via Sweller/Mayer and the broader XR literature, and is now attributed that way.
4. The chapter omitted that the **360° video group also learned** (62.1% vs 43.2% pre-test), omitted the **combined condition**, and omitted the authors' **combine-the-media recommendation** — all now in the opener and the hold-the-result paragraph.
5. The pre-training idea was presented as the study's "own practical recommendation"; it is a Discussion suggestion citing Howard & Lee (2020), and is now cited that way.
6. The "18 of 33" review venue corrected to ***Emerging Science Journal*** (April 2024), with the screened-from-172 note (this finding of the report stands as written).
