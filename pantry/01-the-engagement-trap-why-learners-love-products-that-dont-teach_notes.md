## A. Conceptual foundations

### Engagement is multidimensional, not synonymous with learning
A learner can click often, enjoy the experience, and still fail to encode or transfer the intended knowledge. Treat engagement as behavioral participation, affective response, cognitive investment, and achievement-linked academic engagement. Avoid using time-on-task, completion, ratings, or streaks as learning proof unless they are tied to delayed performance or transfer.

**Common misconception:** More clicks, longer sessions, higher ratings, or completion automatically mean learning.

**Worked example:** Use the studio project. Ask what design decision changes if this concept is true. Then create a before/after artifact and a one-sentence Evidence Disclosure. For this chapter, the simplest case is: Top-rated app with high satisfaction but weak retention evidence.

### Seductive details can increase interest while reducing transfer
Seductive details are interesting additions not needed for the learning goal. They can increase curiosity or liking while diverting attention, activating irrelevant schemas, or disrupting coherence. The design implication is relevance discipline: every delightful element must support the target processing.

**Common misconception:** Making material more interesting is always instructionally helpful.

**Worked example:** Use the studio project. Ask what design decision changes if this concept is true. Then create a before/after artifact and a one-sentence Evidence Disclosure. For this chapter, the simplest case is: Top-rated app with high satisfaction but weak retention evidence.

**Source(s):** See priority sources below.

### Desirable difficulties often feel worse while improving retention
A desirable difficulty makes learning feel harder now because it induces processing that improves later retrieval or transfer. The same surface friction can be desirable or harmful depending on prior knowledge, task complexity, timing, and feedback.

**Common misconception:** All difficulty is good, or all friction is bad.

**Worked example:** Use the studio project. Ask what design decision changes if this concept is true. Then create a before/after artifact and a one-sentence Evidence Disclosure. For this chapter, the simplest case is: Top-rated app with high satisfaction but weak retention evidence.

### Effect sizes describe average differences, not certainty for a context
Effect sizes describe the magnitude of a difference between conditions, not a guarantee. Hedges g is useful for small-sample correction and meta-analytic comparison, but heterogeneity and confidence intervals matter.

**Common misconception:** An effect size is a guarantee for this product and this learner.

## E. Teaching considerations

**Likely sticking point:** Students will tend to turn this chapter into a checklist. Force them to name the evidence status, the learner data source, and the decision that changes.

**Exercise ideas:**
- Diagnose a flawed artifact from the opening case and label the evidence problem.
- Apply the chapter method to the Track A statistics course.
- Track B: apply the method to the student's own project and write a short Evidence Disclosure.

**LLM guardrail:** Permit AI to generate alternatives or critique clarity, but require the student to provide the evidence label, learner-data source, and final design judgment. Do not let AI replace learner contact, delayed assessment, or ethical review.
