# PART 1 — BOOK CONCEPT AND THESIS

## Book concept summary

> This book teaches **evidence-disciplined learning experience design —
> the capability to design, prototype, instrument, and evaluate learning
> experiences that demonstrably produce learning rather than merely
> engagement** — to **graduate students entering LXD, learning
> engineering, and EdTech design roles**, by **walking one real learning
> experience through the full human-centered design process (learner
> research, journey mapping, co-design, prototyping, instrumentation,
> evaluation), with the current empirical evidence base applied as a
> design constraint at every phase**. It fills the gap left by
> practitioner philosophy texts with no evidence discipline (Floor) and
> learning-science texts with no design method (*Make It Stick*),
> neither of which can address the AI era. It succeeds if the reader
> can **take a real learning problem from learner research through a
> tested, instrumented prototype, defend every major design decision
> with evidence — including correctly rejecting an engaging design the
> evidence says will not teach — and report a qualified evaluation in
> two registers**.

**One-sentence logline:**
The market selects for engagement; this course teaches the designer
to select for learning — and to prove the difference.

## Central thesis

"This book argues that learner engagement and learning effectiveness
are empirically separable — engaging design can actively reduce
learning (seductive details, the AI crutch effect) and effective
design can feel worse in the moment (desirable difficulties) — which
means every learning experience design decision is an empirical claim
that must survive the evidence, and this matters because the EdTech
market demonstrably selects for engagement over learning (the EVER
finding), and generative AI is widening the gap faster than design
practice is closing it."

## Thesis test

The TOC reflects the thesis at every act:

- ACT ONE: The student experiences the enjoyable ≠ effective problem
  before acquiring vocabulary for it — a beloved app that doesn't
  teach, a 17%-worse AI tutor — then learns the cognitive and
  motivational machinery that explains the separation. ✓
- ACT TWO: Every design method (research, mapping, co-design,
  prototyping, UDL, gamification, immersion) is taught WITH its
  evidence constraint attached. The student cannot learn the method
  without learning when the evidence says not to use it. ✓
- ACT THREE: The student instruments and evaluates their own design,
  distinguishing behavioral engagement from cognitive engagement from
  learning, and produces a qualified conclusion in two registers. ✓

**Thesis test: PASS**

# PART 2 — LEARNER PROFILE

## Primary reader

A first-year graduate student in a learning design, experience
design, or educational technology master's program — the CMU METALS
or Northeastern CAMD profile. They arrive with one of two halves:
a design background (UX, product, visual) with no learning science,
or a teaching background with no design method.

**Specific person:** A second-year MS student who redesigned an
onboarding course last semester. Completion rates rose, satisfaction
scores were excellent, and three months later the performance data
showed no one had retained anything. They want to know what they
measured, what they should have measured, and what they should have
designed instead.

## Prior knowledge assumed

- Bachelor's-level writing and critical reading
- Basic familiarity with digital learning products as a USER
  (LMS, MOOCs, Duolingo-class apps)
- EITHER design process exposure OR classroom teaching experience
  (the course is built to onboard each half into the other)

## Prior knowledge NOT assumed

- Learning science or educational psychology
- Statistics beyond arithmetic (effect-size literacy is taught)
- Prototyping tools (Figma, Storyline — taught tool-agnostically)
- Programming or data analysis
- Instructional design certifications (ADDIE etc.)

## Prior misconceptions (what they think they know that is wrong)

1. "Engaging design produces learning" — the seductive details
   literature and the EVER finding show the market's most loved
   products often teach least
2. "If learners rate it highly, it worked" — high post-course ratings
   are not evidence of learning; effective designs often feel worse
   in the moment (desirable difficulties)
3. "More personalization is always better" — adaptive systems can
   reproduce inequity as digital tracking; the OECD warning
4. "AI tutoring helps learning" — Bastani et al. (2025): students
   using a GPT tutor scored 17% worse on subsequent non-AI exams
5. "UDL is settled science" — widely used, increasingly contested;
   the evidence base is weaker than advocates assert
6. "LXD is a new discipline with its own methods" — the bibliometric
   evidence shows ongoing borrowing, not consolidation; honest
   framing is required

## Motivation type

Mixed: professional (they are entering Learning Experience Designer
/ Learning Engineer roles; the BLS-projected growth is in
workforce-tied LXD) and academic (course requirement). Professional
motivation is primary; the course honors it — the terminal
deliverable is a portfolio-grade redesign of a real learning
experience, defensible in a hiring loop.

## Prerequisite map

| Prerequisite | Safe to assume? | If not: where addressed |
|-------------|----------------|------------------------|
| Critical reading / writing | Yes | — |
| Digital learning products as user | Yes | — |
| Design process exposure | Probably (half the cohort) | Weeks 5–8 teach it as primary instruction |
| Teaching/learning intuition | Probably (other half) | Weeks 3–4 teach it as primary instruction |
| Effect-size / evidence literacy | No | Week 1 (primary instruction, then recurring) |
| Learning science | No | Weeks 3–4 (primary instruction) |
| Prototyping tools | No | Week 8, tool-agnostic |
| Statistics / analytics | No | Week 13 teaches the design-relevant layer only |

**Front-loading decision:** Effect-size literacy is the only "No"
prerequisite needed before Act Two — it is taught in Week 1 and
reinforced by the recurring Evidence Brief assignment. Learning
science (Weeks 3–4) and analytics (Week 13) are covered at first
use. No Chapter 0 or appendix required.

# PART 3 — BOOK TYPE AND DEPLOYMENT SPECIFICATION

## Book type

**PRIMARY TYPE:** Course textbook — adopted for a semester,
chapter = week, read in sequence, with a studio project thread
running the full arc.

**NOT:** Practitioner handbook (the studio thread creates sequence
dependency), field-defining monograph (it builds capability, not a
sustained argument — though it carries a thesis), reference work
(the out-of-scope section is too aggressive for a reference).

## Deployment specification

**Primary adoption context:**
Graduate LXD / learning design / educational technology master's
programs, across all three lanes the field currently runs:
experience-design-first studio programs (MFA/MS, Northeastern CAMD
profile), learning-science/HCI-first programs (CMU METALS profile),
and systems/policy programs (M.Des, Harvard LDIT profile). 15-week
semester, one chapter per week.

**Secondary adoption context:**
Upper-level undergraduate HCI or education electives; corporate L&D
teams upskilling instructional designers into LXD roles (Acts One
and Two are the most transferable to non-semester formats).

**What the book is NOT designed for:**
PhD research seminars (not theoretical enough); pure UX programs
(learning outcomes are the constraint, not satisfaction); K-12
teacher preparation (the design-process depth assumes a product
context); self-study without a project (the studio thread is
load-bearing).

**How the TOC signals book type to a faculty reviewer:**
Fifteen chapters, one per week, explicit act labels (Establish /
Build / Apply), three milestone assessments at act transitions,
a studio project thread with weekly deliverables, and a terminal
portfolio deliverable specified in the final chapter. A faculty
member building a 15-week syllabus can map this TOC in under ten
minutes.

# PART 4 — FIELD POSITIONING

## The positioning statement — consolidated

The competitive landscape is clean:

- Floor made the case for LXD as human-centered design. This book
  assumes the case and adds the evidence discipline Floor omits.
- Dirksen teaches design heuristics from learning science. This book
  teaches the full design process, and its evidence base is
  post-2024 — the gamification meta-analyses, the VR counterfinding,
  the AI tutoring RCT.
- *Make It Stick* teaches the science with no design method.
- Cohn & Greer apply UX to online courses only — one delivery mode,
  no measurement layer.
- The Springer 2025 edited volume is a research reader, not a
  teachable arc.

The gap this book fills: no course textbook teaches the
human-centered design process for learning WITH the current
quantitative evidence base attached as a design constraint at every
phase, WITH an instrumentation-and-evaluation layer (xAPI, analytics,
adaptive models), and WITH the AI-era findings no earlier text could
include.

**vs. Brown, Roediger & McDaniel, *Make It Stick*:**

# PART 5 — THREE-ACT LEARNING ARC

## The arc statement

This book takes the reader from **designer (or teacher) who can make
learning experiences people like** to **practitioner who can design,
prototype, instrument, and evaluate experiences that demonstrably
produce learning** by first establishing that engagement and learning
are empirically separable and teaching the machinery that explains
the separation (Act One), then building the design-process toolkit
with the evidence constraint attached at each phase (Act Two), then
deploying the toolkit under real conditions — AI, instrumentation,
evaluation — on the student's own project (Act Three).

## The pebble-in-the-pond opening

Week 1 gives the student a product to react to: a top-rated,
beloved learning app evaluated against evidence-based criteria —
and failing them. By Week 2 the student has chosen their studio
project: one real learning experience (their own course, a product
they use, an instructor-provided case) that they will carry through
all fifteen weeks. Every method in Act Two is applied to that
project the week it is taught. Theory arrives only when the project
makes the student need it.

## Act One — Establish (Weeks 1–4)

**Starting state:** The student believes engagement is evidence of
learning.
**Ending state:** The student can explain why engagement and
learning separate (cognitive load, seductive details, desirable
difficulties, SDT), can read an effect size and a meta-analysis,
and has chosen and profiled their studio project.
**Inciting question:** "If learners love it and rate it highly,
how can it not be working — and what would I have to measure to
know?"
**Act One → Act Two transition:** The student can take a design
feature (a badge, an animation, an AI hint) and state the
evidence-based case for and against it — before having any design
method.

## Act Two — Build (Weeks 5–11)

**Starting state:** The student can critique with evidence but
cannot yet design.
**Ending state:** The student has taken their studio project
through learner research, journey mapping, co-design, prototyping,
a variability audit, and evidence-constrained motivation and
modality decisions — and has a tested prototype.
**Hardest conceptual moment:** Week 9 (Designing for Variability)
— the student must hold two truths at once: accessibility is
non-negotiable AND the UDL evidence base is contested. Designing
under honest uncertainty is the hardest move in the course.
**Act Two → Act Three transition:** The student has a prototype
and can state, for every major design decision, whether it is
grounded in evidence, in learner research, or in an explicit
assumption awaiting measurement.

## Act Three — Apply (Weeks 12–15)

**Starting state:** The student has a defensible prototype and a
list of assumptions awaiting measurement.
**Ending state:** The student can make the AI integration decision
(scaffold vs. crutch), instrument the experience (xAPI, analytics),
evaluate it (distinguishing behavioral from cognitive engagement
from learning), and produce a qualified evaluation in two registers
— technical and stakeholder-facing.
**The transfer test:** For Track B students the transfer is not
hypothetical — it happened during the course. They redesigned a
real experience and instrumented it.

## The studio thread across the arc

| Week | Track A path (instructor's case) | Track B path (own project) |
|------|----------------------------------|----------------------------|
| 2 | Adopt instructor's case (intro statistics online course) | Choose and profile own experience |
| 5 | Learner research on provided data | Own learner research |
| 6 | Journey map of instructor's case | Own journey map |
| 7 | Co-design session, provided learners | Own co-design session |
| 8 | Prototype one redesigned segment | Own prototype |
| 9 | Variability audit of instructor's case | Own variability audit |
| 10 | Gamification decision memo | Own motivation design decision |
| 11 | Modality decision memo | Own modality decision |
| 12 | AI integration decision, provided scenario | Own AI integration decision |
| 13 | Measurement plan for instructor's case | Own measurement plan |
| 14 | Evaluation of provided dataset | Own evaluation (or pilot data) |
| 15 | Full redesign portfolio, instructor's case + own-context section | Own full redesign portfolio |

**Evidence Brief format:** one page; one design-relevant claim; the
best evidence for it, the best evidence against it, and the design
decision the student would make under that uncertainty.

**Track B bonus rule:** Any Studio Assignment submitted on the
student's own project earns 5 bonus points above the base 25,
provided the Evidence Disclosure names one design decision the
evidence constrained AND one the evidence could not settle.

**The Evidence Disclosure:** every Studio Assignment carries a
short disclosure naming which decisions are evidence-grounded,
which are research-grounded, and which are assumptions awaiting
measurement. This is the thesis enforced as a grading mechanic.

# PART 8 — CHAPTER-BY-CHAPTER TOC

---

## ACT ONE — ESTABLISH (Weeks 1–4)

*What this act does: establishes that engagement and learning are
empirically separable, teaches the cognitive and motivational
machinery that explains the separation, and builds the evidence
literacy the rest of the course depends on.*

---

### WEEK 1 — The Engagement Trap: Why Learners Love Products That Don't Teach

**One-line:** Students learn to distinguish engagement evidence from
learning evidence, and to read an effect size — before any design
vocabulary.

**Learning outcomes:**
1. (Understand) Explain the enjoyable ≠ effective problem using the
   seductive details and desirable difficulties literatures.
2. (Apply) Read a reported effect size (Hedges' g) and state what it
   does and does not establish, including heterogeneity.
3. (Analyze) Evaluate one top-rated learning app against
   evidence-based criteria (the EVER routine) and identify where
   ratings and learning value diverge.

**Opening:** A top-rated educational app on the App Store —
millions of downloads, glowing reviews — evaluated against
evidence-based criteria and providing minimal learning value
(the EVER finding). The students are asked: what exactly did the
five stars measure?

**Core content:** Engagement as multidimensional (behavioral,
cognitive, affective, academic); seductive details; desirable
difficulties (spacing, retrieval, interleaving — feels worse, works
better); effect-size literacy; why the market selects for engagement.

**Assessment:** Evidence Brief #1 (30 pts)

**Bridge:** If the market's most loved products don't teach, what
is the discipline that is supposed to prevent this — and is it
actually a discipline yet?

---

### WEEK 2 — Learning Experience Design: The Discipline and Its Borders

**One-line:** Students learn what LXD is, what it borrows from ID,
UX, and service design, and what it uniquely optimizes for — with
the rebranding critique stated honestly, not hidden.

**Learning outcomes:**
1. (Understand) Differentiate LXD from instructional design, UX
   design, and service design by primary question, output, and
   optimization target.
2. (Analyze) Examine the "LXD is rebranded ID" critique and state
   what evidence would settle it.
3. (Apply / Track B) Select and profile a real learning experience
   as their studio project, stating its intended learning outcome
   and its current evidence of effectiveness (usually: none).

**Opening:** One learning product described four times — by an
instructional designer, a UX designer, a service designer, and an
LX designer. Four briefs, four different deliverables, one product.

**Core content:** The disciplinary synthesis (Jahnke, McDonald,
Floor); the four-field comparison table; the Devlin Peck critique;
LXD as emerging discipline — borrowing without consolidation;
experience as the unit of design.

**Assessment:** Studio project selection due (ungraded gate;
required for Week 5)

**Bridge:** LXD claims learning is its outcome constraint. Then the
designer must know how learning actually works. Two weeks of
machinery follow.

---

### WEEK 3 — The Cognitive Machinery: Load, Difficulty, and Durable Learning

**One-line:** Students learn the cognitive architecture that
explains why designs succeed or fail — cognitive load theory,
desirable difficulties, and the constructivist/situated/embodied
foundations — as design-actionable mechanisms, not history.

**Learning outcomes:**
1. (Understand) Classify mental effort as intrinsic, extraneous, or
   germane load for a specified learning task.
2. (Apply) Redesign a provided learning interaction to eliminate
   extraneous load without removing desirable difficulty.
3. (Analyze) Distinguish a desirable difficulty from a design flaw
   in a provided product — the same surface friction, opposite
   verdicts.
4. (Analyze / Track B) Identify the dominant load problem in their
   studio project.

**Opening:** Two versions of the same lesson. Version A is smoother,
prettier, easier — and produces worse retention. The redesign that
"fixed" the lesson broke it.

**Core content:** CLT (intrinsic/extraneous/germane); desirable
difficulties operationalized (spacing, retrieval, interleaving,
generation); constructivism, situated learning, embodied cognition
— each reduced to its design implication; the checklist trap.

**Assessment:** Evidence Brief #2 (30 pts)

**Bridge:** Load explains capacity. It does not explain why anyone
keeps going. Motivation is next — and it is also not what the
badge vendors say it is.

---

### WEEK 4 — Motivation by Design: Self-Determination, Flow, and Task Value
**[MIDTERM: EVIDENCE DIAGNOSTIC]**

**One-line:** Students learn the empirically supported motivation
frameworks — SDT, flow, task-value beliefs — and use them to
predict when engagement mechanics will produce durable motivation
versus novelty decay.

**Learning outcomes:**
1. (Understand) Explain autonomy, competence, and relatedness as
   design requirements, with one design expression of each.
2. (Apply) Diagnose a provided product's motivation design: which
   SDT needs it serves, which it starves.
3. (Analyze) Predict from design structure whether a gamified
   system will sustain motivation or decay after novelty, using the
   intrinsic/extrinsic distinction.
4. (Evaluate) Judge a design against the task-value finding — is
   the personal/professional utility of the content visible to the
   learner by design, or assumed?

**Opening:** A corporate learning platform's leaderboard: usage
spikes for six weeks, collapses to below baseline by week twelve —
and the post-launch survey scores were excellent the whole time.

**Core content:** SDT and its meta-analytic support; flow and its
measurement problems (stated honestly); task-value beliefs as the
strongest engagement predictor; extrinsic-reward decay; motivation
as prerequisite, not endpoint.

**Assessment:** Midterm — Evidence Diagnostic (100 pts): six unseen
design claims; for each, the student states the relevant evidence,
its strength, and the design decision it warrants. + Evidence
Brief #3 due.

**Bridge:** Act One is complete: the student can critique with
evidence. Act Two begins with the first design method — and the
first rule of evidence-disciplined design: research the learner
before designing anything.

## ACT TWO — BUILD (Weeks 5–11)

*What this act does: constructs the design-process toolkit one
method per week, with the evidence constraint attached at each
phase, applied each week to the student's studio project.*

---

### WEEK 5 — Learner Research: Personas, Misconceptions, and Task-Value Mapping

**One-line:** Students learn to research real learners — prior
knowledge, misconceptions, motivation type, task-value perception —
and to distinguish learner research from market research.

**Learning outcomes:**
1. (Apply) Conduct and document learner research using interviews
   and empathy mapping with 3–5 real learners.
2. (Analyze) Extract prior misconceptions — what learners think
   they know that is wrong — as design inputs, not just knowledge
   gaps.
3. (Create) Produce evidence-based learner personas that encode
   prior knowledge, misconception, motivation type, and task-value
   perception (not demographics and stock photos).
4. (Evaluate / Track B) Assess which of their project's current
   design assumptions survive contact with real learners.

**Opening:** Two persona sets for the same product: the marketing
team's (demographics, lifestyle, a name like "Busy Brenda") and a
learning researcher's (prior knowledge, misconception, task-value).
Only one predicts design failure.

**Core content:** Learner research methods; empathy mapping; the
misconception interview; motivation typing; AI-assisted persona
generation — and why it cannot replace contact with real learners.

**Assessment:** Studio Assignment #1 (25/30 pts)

**Bridge:** Research describes the learner at a moment. Learning
happens across time. The next method maps the experience as a
journey — including the parts the content outline cannot see.

---

### WEEK 6 — Mapping the Learning Journey: Experience, Not Content

**One-line:** Students learn to map the full arc of a learner's
experience — cognitive load shifts, emotional states, friction
points, reflection moments — and why a journey map is not a
content outline.

**Learning outcomes:**
1. (Apply) Construct a learner journey map tracking touchpoints,
   load, emotional state, and reflection opportunities.
2. (Analyze) Identify the journey's highest-risk moment — where
   extraneous load, motivation collapse, or dropout concentrates.
3. (Analyze / Track B) Locate where their project's journey
   diverges from its content outline — the experience the syllabus
   doesn't show.
4. (Evaluate) Distinguish friction to remove (extraneous) from
   friction to keep (desirable difficulty) at each mapped point.

**Opening:** A course with a 40% dropout cliff at week three. The
content outline shows nothing unusual at week three. The journey
map shows everything: a tool change, an unscaffolded assignment,
and the first assessment with no prior feedback — stacked on the
same day.

**Core content:** Journey mapping adapted from CX; mapping load
and affect, not just steps; the dropout-cliff diagnostic; service
blueprinting for the actors behind the experience.

**Assessment:** Studio Assignment #2 (25/30 pts)

**Bridge:** The map shows where the experience fails its learners.
Before redesigning it FOR them, the next method asks them to
redesign it WITH them.

---

### WEEK 7 — Co-Design: Learners as Design Partners

**One-line:** Students learn participatory design methods that
engage learners as design partners — and the evidence that
co-design changes both the artifact and the learning relationship.

**Learning outcomes:**
1. (Apply) Plan and facilitate a structured co-design session with
   real learners, with defined roles, artifacts, and a feedback
   matrix.
2. (Analyze) Distinguish co-design from requirements-gathering and
   from focus-group theater — participation that shapes decisions
   versus participation that decorates them.
3. (Evaluate) Judge which design decisions should be opened to
   learners and which require design or evidence authority —
   learners reliably want less desirable difficulty.
4. (Create / Track B) Integrate co-design outputs into a revised
   design direction with an explicit record of what was accepted,
   rejected, and why.

**Opening:** A co-design session where learners unanimously vote
to remove the practice testing. The designer's dilemma in one
room: the learners are the experts on their experience and wrong
about their learning.

**Core content:** Participatory design lineage; postdigital
co-design findings; structured feedback matrices; the
authority-boundary problem; co-design with minors and vulnerable
populations (ethics).

**Assessment:** Studio Assignment #3 (25/30 pts)

**Bridge:** Research, map, and co-design direction in hand — the
next move is making something testable, fast, before investing in
making it polished.

---

### WEEK 8 — Prototyping Learning Experiences: From Paper to Pilot
**[PROTOTYPE CHECKPOINT]**

**One-line:** Students learn rapid prototyping and design-sprint
methods adapted to learning experiences — testing information
flows and learning interactions before production fidelity.

**Learning outcomes:**
1. (Apply) Produce a low-fidelity prototype of one redesigned
   segment of the learning experience.
2. (Apply) Run a structured prototype test with real learners,
   capturing both usability findings AND learning-relevant
   findings (load, misconception triggers, motivation).
3. (Analyze) Separate usability failures from learning-design
   failures in test results — the fix is different.
4. (Evaluate) Decide what fidelity the next iteration requires,
   and justify the cost.

**Opening:** A team that spent four months building a polished
branching simulation, then watched the first real learner
misunderstand the core mechanic in ninety seconds. The paper
version would have caught it in week one.

**Core content:** Fidelity ladders; the 5-day sprint adapted to
curriculum; testing learning interactions vs. testing interfaces;
prototyping with authoring tools vs. paper; what cannot be
prototyped (durable retention) and must wait for measurement.

**Assessment:** Prototype Checkpoint (100 pts) — prototype +
test report + Evidence Disclosure. Track switch decision point.

**Bridge:** The prototype works for the learners who were in the
room. The next three weeks stress-test it against the learners
who weren't: the variability audit, the motivation decision, the
modality decision.

---

### WEEK 9 — Designing for Variability: UDL, Accessibility, and the Equity Test

**One-line:** Students learn to design for learner variability from
the start — UDL 3.0, accessibility standards, personalization's
equity risks — while holding the honest position that parts of the
UDL evidence base are contested.

**Learning outcomes:**
1. (Apply) Audit a learning experience against UDL 3.0 guidelines
   and applicable accessibility standards (WCAG).
2. (Analyze) Distinguish designing for variability from adaptive
   tracking — and explain how personalization can reproduce
   inequity ("digital tracking," the OECD warning).
3. (Evaluate) Assess which UDL-derived design changes are supported
   by evidence, which are accessibility obligations independent of
   learning-outcome evidence, and which are contested — and decide
   anyway, with the uncertainty documented.
4. (Create / Track B) Produce a variability redesign of one segment
   of their project with the evidence status of each change labeled.

**Opening:** An adaptive platform that routes struggling students
into remedial drills — and the cohort data showing who gets routed,
who advances to the creative tasks, and how the gap compounds over
a semester. The personalization worked exactly as designed.

**Core content:** UDL 3.0 (nine guidelines); the contested-evidence
critique stated fairly; accessibility as obligation vs. UDL as
learning claim — different justification logics; personalization
vs. equity; culturally sustaining and trauma-informed complements;
human-centered learning analytics as the alternative framing.

**Assessment:** Studio Assignment #4 (25/30 pts)

**Bridge:** The design now serves more learners. The next two weeks
ask the seductive questions: should it be a game? Should it be
immersive? The evidence has strong opinions.

### WEEK 10 — Gamification Under Evidence: Motivation Mechanics That Survive the Meta-Analyses

**One-line:** Students learn to make the gamification decision the
way the evidence demands: moderately positive on average
(g = 0.782), heterogeneous underneath, SDT-aligned designs durable,
points-badges-leaderboards decaying.

**Learning outcomes:**
1. (Analyze) Decompose a gamified product into its mechanics and
   classify each as serving autonomy, competence, relatedness, or
   pure extrinsic reward.
2. (Evaluate) Judge whether gamification is warranted for a
   specified learning context, citing the meta-analytic evidence
   including its negative-effect studies and the
   raises-the-ceiling-more-than-the-floor finding.
3. (Create) Design (or explicitly decline to design) a motivation
   mechanic for the studio project, with an Evidence Disclosure
   covering decay risk.

**Opening:** Two gamified deployments of the same curriculum. One
sustains gains across three years (the longitudinal finding); one
collapses after novelty. The difference is not the badges — it is
which psychological needs the mechanics feed.

**Core content:** The gamification evidence base (Zeng et al. 2024;
Lampropoulos & Sidiropoulos 2024); heterogeneity as the headline;
SDT-aligned mechanic design; extrinsic-reward decay and motivational
crowding; when the right answer is "no game."

**Assessment:** Studio Assignment #5 (25/30 pts) + Evidence Brief #4
(30 pts)

**Bridge:** Gamification changes the mechanics of the experience.
The next decision changes its medium — and the evidence there
contains the course's sharpest counterfinding.

### WEEK 11 — Immersive and Embodied Learning: When the Medium Earns Its Cost

**One-line:** Students learn when VR/AR and embodied interaction
genuinely improve learning (spatial knowledge, procedural rehearsal,
functionally aligned action) and when they add extraneous load and
novelty distraction — including the touch-tank counterfinding.

**Learning outcomes:**
1. (Understand) Explain the two mechanisms by which immersion can
   reduce learning: interface-induced extraneous load and the
   novelty effect.
2. (Analyze) Evaluate a proposed XR learning experience against the
   moderating evidence: immersion level, equipment type, prior XR
   experience, adaptive layering.
3. (Evaluate) Make a defensible modality decision for a specified
   learning task, including the pre-training requirement when
   immersion is chosen.
4. (Evaluate / Track B) Make and disclose the modality decision for
   their studio project.

**Opening:** A hands-on oyster-reef lesson (live touch tanks) versus
a headset 360° video of the same habitat (Walters et al. 2026):
both teach, but hands-on outscores the video by 31% — the chapter
supplies the load-and-novelty mechanism theory the gap demands, and
carries the study's own conclusion that combining the media is best.

**Core content:** The XR evidence (moderate average effects, strong
moderators); embodied cognition — functionally aligned action vs.
decorative gesture; the pre-training prescription; adaptive VR; the
modality decision framework; cost as a design variable.

**Assessment:** Studio Assignment #6 (25/30 pts)

**Bridge:** Act Two is complete: a researched, mapped, co-designed,
tested, audited prototype with defended motivation and modality
decisions. Act Three deploys it into the conditions that decide
its fate: AI, measurement, and evaluation.

### WEEK 12 — AI in the Learning Experience: Scaffold or Crutch

**One-line:** Students learn to make AI integration decisions
against the field's cautionary benchmark — the 17%-worse RCT —
distinguishing AI that preserves productive struggle from AI that
bypasses it.

**Learning outcomes:**
1. (Understand) Explain the crutch effect: how a responsive,
   well-liked AI tutor produced worse learning, and which design
   properties caused it.
2. (Analyze) Classify AI integrations (feedback at scale, adaptive
   difficulty, conversational tutoring, agentic pathways) by their
   evidence status and their productive-struggle risk.
3. (Evaluate) Assess an AI-mediated design for equity risk —
   including the finding that learners with executive-function
   challenges are most likely to become dependent.
4. (Create / Track B) Produce the AI integration decision for their
   studio project: what AI does, what it is forbidden to do, and
   the guardrail design — with Evidence Disclosure.

**Opening:** The Bastani RCT, told as an experience: the students
liked the GPT tutor, used it heavily, felt helped — and scored 17%
worse when it was taken away. Every individual interaction was
satisfying. The aggregate was damage.

**Core content:** The AI evidence map (strong: formative feedback
at scale, adaptive difficulty; thin: almost everything else — 20
high-quality causal studies in 800+ papers); the crutch effect;
guardrail design for productive struggle; agentic AI and the
governance lag; designer deskilling as a parallel risk.

**Assessment:** Studio Assignment #7 (25/30 pts) + Evidence Brief #5
(30 pts)

**Bridge:** Every decision so far rests on assumptions awaiting
measurement. The next week builds the measurement — and the
instrument decides what the designer is allowed to learn.

### WEEK 13 — Instrumenting the Experience: xAPI, Analytics, and Adaptive Models
**[MEASUREMENT PLAN CHECKPOINT]**

**One-line:** Students learn to instrument a learning experience —
xAPI/LRS over legacy SCORM, behavioral analytics, and the adaptive
modeling layer (IRT, Bayesian risk models) — as designers who must
specify what to measure, not engineers who implement it.

**Learning outcomes:**
1. (Understand) Explain what xAPI tracks that SCORM cannot, and why
   that difference determines which design questions are answerable.
2. (Apply) Specify an instrumentation plan: which interactions emit
   statements, which metrics distinguish behavioral from cognitive
   engagement, which proxy learning.
3. (Analyze) Interpret IRT difficulty calibration and Bayesian
   at-risk output as design inputs — and name what each model
   cannot tell the designer.
4. (Create / Track B) Produce the measurement plan for their own
   project, mapping each open design assumption from Weeks 5–12 to
   the data that would test it.

**Opening:** Two dashboards for the same course. The SCORM dashboard:
completion 94%, quiz average 88% — everything green. The xAPI
dashboard: pause-and-rewind clusters, hint-exhaustion patterns,
practice-skipping — the failure that the green dashboard had no
vocabulary to show.

**Core content:** xAPI/LRS architecture for designers; metric
design — behavioral vs. cognitive engagement vs. learning; time-on-
task as a trap; IRT and Bayesian models at design-literacy depth;
measurement ethics and learner data dignity.

**Assessment:** Measurement Plan Checkpoint (100 pts) + Studio
Assignment #8 (25/30 pts)

**Bridge:** The instruments are specified. The last analytical move
is the honest one: deciding what the results mean, what they don't,
and what to tell the people who weren't in the room.

### WEEK 14 — Evaluating Learning Experience Quality: Beyond the Smile Sheet

**One-line:** Students learn mixed-model evaluation — experience
indicators, participation metrics, and learning outcomes together —
and produce a qualified conclusion that survives both a technical
review and a stakeholder meeting.

**Learning outcomes:**
1. (Apply) Apply a mixed-model evaluation framework (OLC-scorecard
   class) to a learning experience: design, engagement, assessment,
   accessibility.
2. (Analyze) Diagnose an evaluation that mistakes satisfaction for
   learning — and name what additional evidence would be required
   to claim effectiveness.
3. (Evaluate) Produce a qualified conclusion in two registers:
   technical (what the data supports, the confounds, the limits)
   and stakeholder-facing (the decision recommendation in plain
   language, without overclaiming).
4. (Evaluate / Track B) Evaluate their own project's pilot data (or
   the provided dataset) and state honestly which design assumptions
   were tested, which survived, and which remain open.

**Opening:** A program with a 71-point NPS and no learning gains —
presented to a leadership team that has already drafted the
expansion announcement. The student's task: write the evaluation
that tells the truth without being ignored.

**Core content:** Mixed-model evaluation; the ROI measurement gap
(71% rate it important, 46% measure it, most reduce it to hours
saved); confound honesty; the two-register conclusion; when the
honest answer is "not definitive — and here is what would settle it."

**Assessment:** Studio Assignment #9 (25/30 pts)

**Bridge:** Every artifact exists. Week 15 assembles them into the
one deliverable that matters: the full redesign, every decision
explicit, defensible in a hiring loop or a budget meeting.

### WEEK 15 — The Full Redesign: One Experience, Every Decision
**[FINAL PROJECT]**

**One-line:** Students produce a complete redesign portfolio for a
real learning experience — research through evaluation, every
design decision evidence-disclosed, qualified conclusions in two
registers, defensible to a skeptical reviewer.

**Learning outcomes:**
1. (Create) Produce a complete redesign portfolio: learner research,
   journey map, co-design record, prototype with test results,
   variability audit, motivation/modality/AI decisions, measurement
   plan, and evaluation.
2. (Evaluate) Review a peer's portfolio using the course's evidence
   diagnostic before final presentation.
3. (Create) Defend the redesign to a skeptical reviewer — including
   correctly defending at least one decision to NOT add an engaging
   feature the evidence argued against.

**Opening:** What does "done" look like? The instructor's complete
Track A portfolio — the statistics course redesign built in
segments across Weeks 12–14 — presented whole, with its own
Evidence Disclosures, including the decisions that remain open.

**Core content:** Portfolio structure; peer review protocol; the
defense format; what "evidence-disciplined" means as a professional
identity rather than a course requirement.

**Assessment:** Final Redesign Portfolio (250 pts) — Track A:
instructor's case + required own-context section. Track B: own
project complete.

**Final Evidence Disclosure (higher bar):** must name three design
decisions across the full arc where the evidence overruled the
designer's (or the learners') preference, and one where the
evidence could not decide and judgment did.

# PART 9 — CHAPTER ANATOMY TEMPLATE

All 15 chapters follow this structure:

1. Learning objectives (Bloom's level explicit; Track A/B split
   where levels differ)
2. Opening case (failure-first or counterfinding-first; sourced or
   labeled illustrative)
3. Prerequisites stated as specific capabilities
4. Core content sections (4–6), each: concept → evidence → design
   application
5. Mid-chapter checkpoint (ungraded; surfaces confusion before the
   worked example)
6. The Evidence Box (every chapter: the key studies, effect sizes,
   heterogeneity, and what remains unsettled — the thesis as
   recurring apparatus)
7. Worked example (Act One: diagnostic case; Act Two: method applied
   to the Track A statistics course; Act Three: segment of the
   instructor's continuing redesign)
8. Track B extension in the worked example (Weeks 5–14)
9. Assessable exercises (minimum 3; at least one at Apply or above;
   at least one Track B version)
10. Evidence Disclosure template (per-chapter version of the
    grading mechanic)
11. Chapter summary (capabilities gained, not topics covered)
12. Key terms (5–10; plain-language definitions)
13. Bridge question (one question; raises what the next chapter
    answers)
14. Further reading (3–5 sources with one-sentence annotation)
15. LLM Exercise (copy-paste-ready prompt; produces an assessable
    artifact; includes a crutch-effect guardrail consistent with
    Week 12)

**Enforcement:** A draft chapter missing items 5, 6, 7, 13, or 15
is an incomplete draft. Do not advance to peer review without
resolving it.

## Case escalation

Act One cases: single concept, clear diagnostic answer.
Act Two cases: multiple concepts, judgment required; method applied
to the Track A course each week.
Act Three cases: one continuing case across four chapters — the
instructor's full statistics-course redesign — full synthesis, open
decisions shown honestly.

## Worked example format (all chapters)

Situation → the problem as the designer sees it → process (including
dead ends) → resolution → the lesson (one sentence) → the limit
(where this approach fails) → Track B extension (Weeks 5–14).

## Failure-case requirement

At least five chapter openings are documented failures or
counterfindings (Weeks 1, 4, 8, 11, 12). Failure cases teach
diagnostic reasoning; success cases teach pattern matching.

## Sourcing requirement

Every case requires either a source citation OR an explicit
"illustrative case" label. Neither is optional. The headline
findings (EVER, Bastani RCT, touch-tank counterfinding, gamification
meta-analyses, longitudinal gamification study) are sourced to the
primary literature in the Evidence Boxes.

# PART 11 — HARD TOPICS, CONTESTED CLAIMS, AGING RISK

## Contested claims summary

| Claim | Status | Book's position |
|-------|--------|----------------|
| LXD is a distinct discipline | Disputed | Honest framing: emerging, borrowing without consolidation; the rebranding critique presented fairly (Week 2) |
| UDL improves learning outcomes | Contested | Accessibility as obligation regardless; UDL learning claims labeled by evidence status; complements named (Week 9) |
| Gamification works | Heterogeneous | g = 0.782 average WITH negative-effect studies named; SDT-alignment as the moderator (Week 10) |
| Immersion improves learning | Conditional | Moderate effects with strong moderators; the 31% hands-on-vs-360°-video counterfinding (Walters et al. 2026: single study, n = 79, whose authors recommend combining the media) taught, not buried (Week 11) |
| AI tutoring helps learning | Cautionary | The 17%-worse RCT as benchmark; "currently" qualifier on all AI claims; scaffold/crutch as the design frame (Week 12) |
| Enjoyable ≠ effective is resolvable by measurement | Open | Cognitive vs. behavioral engagement as best current practice; measurement frameworks not yet standardized — stated as an open problem (Weeks 13–14) |

## Hard chapters

**Week 9 (Designing for Variability):** The student must act under
honest uncertainty — accessibility non-negotiable, UDL evidence
contested. Requires an author or reviewer with accessibility
expertise; the chapter fails if it reads as either UDL advocacy or
UDL debunking. Expert review required (Risk 6).

**Week 12 (AI):** The fastest-aging chapter and the one carrying
the book's most cited finding. Must be structured stable-core /
current-state: the crutch-effect mechanism and guardrail design
logic are stable; the tool landscape and study citations are not.

**Week 13 (Instrumenting):** The quantitative depth problem.
Design-literacy treatment of IRT and Bayesian models without
becoming an analytics course — the most common failure is teaching
the math instead of the design decision. Requires an author who has
specified instrumentation for a real product.

| Content type | Risk | Review cadence |
|-------------|------|----------------|
| AI findings and tool references (Week 12) | High | Before each offering |
| Named platforms and authoring tools | High | Before each offering |
| Salary/market data (instructor's manual) | High | Annually |
| UDL guideline versions | Medium | On CAST releases |
| Meta-analytic effect sizes | Medium | Every 2 years |
| xAPI/LRS architecture | Low-Medium | Every 2–3 years |
| CLT, SDT, desirable difficulties, journey mapping, co-design method | Low | On major new results |

# PART 12 — MARKET POSITIONING SUMMARY

The gap this book fills: no course textbook teaches the
human-centered design process for learning with the post-2024
evidence base attached as a design constraint at every phase, with
an instrumentation-and-evaluation layer, and with the AI-era
findings — in a studio-threaded, 15-week, milestone-assessed arc a
faculty member can adopt in ten minutes.

Five comparable texts analyzed in Part 4. The competitive position
is clean: Floor has the philosophy without the evidence; Dirksen
has heuristics without the process or the post-2024 findings;
*Make It Stick* has science without design; Cohn & Greer have one
delivery mode; the Springer volume is a reader, not a course.

**Market size estimate:**
Graduate LXD/learning design/EdTech programs across the three lanes:
40–80 course adoptions per year at steady state.
15–30 students per adoption.
1,200–2,500 copies annually at steady state.
Secondary market (corporate L&D upskilling, undergraduate HCI
electives): ~25–40% additional.
Growth driver: the field's job-market split favors exactly the
evidence-plus-product profile this book trains.
